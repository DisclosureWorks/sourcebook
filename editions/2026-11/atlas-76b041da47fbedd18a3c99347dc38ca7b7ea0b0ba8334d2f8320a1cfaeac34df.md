# Charter Legislation (1978–1980)

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The failed Carter-era effort to enact a **comprehensive statutory charter for the Intelligence Community** — the post-Church-Pike successor reform that the Church Committee had explicitly recommended.[^1] The principal vehicles were:

- **National Intelligence Reorganization and Reform Act of 1978** — S. 2525 (95th Cong., 2d Sess. 1978); identical House bill H.R. 11,245 (95th Cong., 2d Sess. 1978).[^2]
- **S. 2284** (96th Cong., 2d Sess. 1980) — successor revision.[^3]

After **almost four years of legislative proceedings**, the Congress and the Carter administration could not reach agreement on a comprehensive proposal for the Intelligence Community's structure.[^4] Per [Bruemmer (1992)](https://openyls.law.yale.edu/entities/publication/f24d637a-5286-41ea-8701-e0ca63f4bc44) (n. 53–54): "the Administration ended up opposing efforts to codify many of the provisions of its own Executive order on intelligence activities."[^5]

## Overview

The Charter Legislation effort was the Church Committee's principal reform recommendation.[^6] Per Church Committee Final Report Book I:[^7]

> "[I]t is the Committee's view that effective congressional oversight requires the power to authorize the budgets of the national intelligence agencies. Without such authority, an oversight committee may find itself in possession of important secret information but unable to act effectively to protect the principles, integrity, and reputation of the United States."[^7]

The proposed Charter would have[^8]:

- Created statutory charters for a number of Intelligence Community agencies[^8]
- Codified Covert Action authorities and limitations[^9]
- Established statutory budget-authorization mechanisms[^10]
- (Church Committee recommendation, 1976, not confirmed as bill content:) statutory bars on paid or contractual relationships with journalists accredited to U.S. media organizations and on the operational use of people who regularly contribute to U.S. media, and a statutory bar on any paid or contractual relationship with an American clergyman or missionary (Recs. 46–48) and steps to protect academic institutions (Recs. 42–44)[^11]
- (per the Church Committee's recommendations, not confirmed as bill content) prohibited specified classes of covert activity by statute[^12]
- (per the Church Committee's recommendations, not confirmed as bill content) made the CIA the only agency authorized to conduct covert action[^13]

The bills would have codified many provisions of the executive framework that Carter set out in **EO 12036** (1978), an order the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence had helped formulate.[^14] But — per Bruemmer (1992) — when Congress moved to codify those EO provisions in statutory form, the Carter administration **opposed many of the very provisions it had voluntarily adopted internally**.[^15]

## Historical significance

Bruemmer reports that the Carter administration opposed codifying many provisions of its own intelligence executive order; Executive Order 12333 later revoked Executive Order 12036.[^16]

The legislative sequence:
1. **Crisis** — Church Committee Final Report (1976) exposed CIA/FBI/NSA abuses requiring statutory remedy.[^17]
2. **Proposal** — National Intelligence Reorganization and Reform Act of 1978 (S. 2525) provided comprehensive Charter framework.[^18]
3. **Negotiation** — Almost four years of legislative proceedings, beginning with the 1978 bill and continuing with its 1980 successor.[^19]
4. **Failure** — Carter administration opposed codification of many of its own EO 12036 provisions[^20].
5. **Substitute** — [Intelligence Oversight Act of 1980](atlas-57fbb56c98455e69a6a52b3b6c5a4fff423bc09eb5ac43701cbee952f9162362.md) enacted as the **partial substitute** (a new Title V added to the National Security Act of 1947 by § 407(b)(1) of the Intelligence Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 1981) — a procedural-framework statute, not the comprehensive substantive Charter the Church Committee had proposed.[^21]

The IOA-1980 substitute is the framework that [McCormick & Smith (1987)](https://doi.org/10.2307/419267) documented failing in the Iran-Contra episode seven years later.[^22] Whether comprehensive Charter Legislation would have closed McCormick & Smith's later-identified ambiguities (NSC-staff loophole, "due regard" clause, "timely fashion" clause, "significant" qualifier) remains a research hypothesis: Congress and the administration did not agree on a comprehensive proposal,[^25] and the cited sources do not establish that counterfactual.

[Bruemmer (1992)](https://openyls.law.yale.edu/entities/publication/f24d637a-5286-41ea-8701-e0ca63f4bc44) cites the Charter Legislation failure as the cautionary tale justifying his alternative procedural framework (Defense Base Closure Act of 1990 model — the executive proposes, and the plan takes effect unless Congress passes a joint resolution of disapproval within 45 days).[^23] Bruemmer's argument: had the Charter Legislation been considered under the Base Closure procedure, "hard choices and difficult compromises will not result in extended delay or failure."[^24]

## Open Questions

- At what legislative stage, and through which decisions, did the comprehensive charter effort end?

- What were the specific substantive provisions of S. 2525 (1978) that the Carter administration opposed during negotiations?
- Were the Church Committee’s proposed protections for journalists, clergy, and academic institutions subsequently enacted in another statutory vehicle?
- Is the Charter Legislation failure properly described as a Carter-era instance of the [executive-branch counter-mobilization](atlas-ba0864ef2c3382b1d49623dabc1f304edce8f13c791c4c7842eca567dd150e12.md) reform-recapture pattern?
