# Security Commission Pattern

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** Kitts (1996) describes Ford’s appointment of the Rockefeller Commission as a preemptive maneuver designed to outflank Congress.[^1] Kitts (1996) presents a case study of the <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Rockefeller Commission</span> (1975), identifies the Doolittle and Katzenbach commissions as precedents, and notes several later commissions;[^29] it does not establish a six-commission comparative pattern.

## Overview

Standard scholarship on presidential commissions (Marcy 1945, Popper 1970, Wolanin 1975, Sulzner 1971, Flitner 1986) offers generally uncritical analysis of commissions; Wolanin, for example, concluded that most advisory panels do a reasonably good job of aiding policy formation through thorough fact-finding and analysis.[^2] Wolanin (1975): "There appears to be very little muck to rake on the subject of independence of commissions"[^3] . . . "they are not packed, and they are not manipulated through White House liaison"[^3] . . . "The requirements of credibility preclude packing and interference."[^3]

Kitts (1996), in a single case study, argues that fact-finding and analysis was not the primary concern in Ford’s decision to appoint the Rockefeller Commission; he describes its appointment as a **preemptive maneuver** designed to outflank Congress and **issue management** as a key objective, while warning against generalizing from one case.[^4] In the Rockefeller case, the cited sources describe[^6][^7][^9]:

1. **Narrow mandate.** The Rockefeller Commission's mandate covered "domestic activity" and "technical compliance with the law".[^6]
2. **Personnel weighted toward intelligence/national-security-establishment sympathies.** In the Rockefeller case, commission membership included members with strong ties to the intelligence community and one member with known sympathies for the agency being investigated, and its executive director had served on the staff of the Warren Commission.[^7] On the Rockefeller Commission, cultural diversity was remarkably low: all members were white men over fifty, whereas the 1967 Kerner Commission had included two African Americans and a woman.[^8]
3. **Reform recapture.** Kitts (1996) argues that the Rockefeller Commission largely adopted Ford’s framing of Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) abuses as isolated incidents without presidential authorization, supporting his opposition to an expanded congressional oversight role.[^9]

## Analytical significance

**Commissions discussed in the cited sources:**[^29][^23]

| Year | Commission | Context (per sources) | Outcome (per cited sources) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1954 | **Doolittle Commission** (operated in complete secrecy)[^10] | Sen. Joseph McCarthy threats to investigate CIA[^11] | Successfully avoided congressional inquiry[^12] |
| 1967 | **Katzenbach Commission** (no "citizen" members; high-level inter-agency task force)[^13] | Potential congressional inquiry into CIA funding of the National Student Association[^14] | Successfully limited investigation to executive branch[^15] |
| 1975 | **<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Rockefeller Commission</span>**[^16] | Hersh New York Times (NYT) story → emerging Church/Pike investigations[^17] | congressional investigations proceeded but limited reform achieved[^18] |
| 1983[^19] | **Kissinger Commission** on Central America[^20] | — | Kitts notes this commission among later national-security commissions; the cited sources do not establish that it provided cover for covert operations |
| 1986 | **Tower Commission** on Iran-Contra[^21] | Congressional Iran-Contra investigations[^22] | Kitts identifies it as an Iran-Contra commission; the cited sources do not characterize the report as reducing a constitutional crisis to procedural failure |
| 1995–96 | **Aspin-Brown Commission** on intelligence community (IC) roles and capabilities (*Preparing for the 21st Century*)[^23] | Bansemer (2006) reports that Congress continued examining the intelligence community in October 1994 amid post-Cold War reform pressure, partly because of a perceived lack of executive-branch will to make changes.[^24] | Bansemer describes a “relatively limited effect of reform” and quotes a commission staff member’s assessment that the intelligence community was “largely unaffected” by the inquiry.[^28] |

The Doolittle and Katzenbach examples are not formally "presidential commissions" in the standard sense (no citizen members; operated under secrecy); Kitts nonetheless treats both as precedents for using an ad hoc group to protect the intelligence community from congressional action.[^25]

## Open Questions

- How far has the security commission hypothesis been tested across post-1947 commissions, including Aspin-Brown, beyond the cases discussed by Kitts and Olmsted?
- What criteria distinguish containment from fact-finding, and how do executive cooperation, staff independence and subsequent reform proposals compare across commissions such as Rockefeller and the 9/11 Commission?
- Is the hypothesis's predictive force conditional on a hostile, defensive executive posture, and how could the Warren Commission or other cases test whether cooperation changes the outcome?
- What prompted the creation of the Kissinger Commission on Central America, and did congressional pressure on the Reagan administration's Central America policy play a part?
