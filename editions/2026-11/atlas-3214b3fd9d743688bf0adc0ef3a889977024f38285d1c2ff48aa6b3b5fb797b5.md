# Mossad Al Unit

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Secondary-source summary. This page summarises published books and memoirs (Whitney Webb, Victor Ostrovsky, Gordon Thomas and Martin Dillon, Ari Ben-Menashe, Israel Shahak). Allegations are attributed to their authors and are not independently established by primary records held in this archive. Not everything on this page is footnoted. **Definition:** In *By Way of Deception*, Chapter 14, PDF p. 277 / printed p. 269, Ostrovsky describes a secret Mossad division whose activities were **mainly, but not exclusively, within the United States**.[^1] Its existence, structure and covert activities are claims in his historical account, not a verified description of current operations.[^1]

## Overview

<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Victor Ostrovsky</span> describes Al as distinct from ordinary Mossad stations in *By Way of Deception*, **Chapter 14, printed pp. 269–286 / PDF pp. 277–294**.[^2] The following practices are the memoir’s account:[^2]

- Al katsas use **American passports** and the cover of the country they work in.[^3]
- Al operates from **safe houses in New York and Washington** rather than embassy installations.[^4]
- Al reports **directly to the head of the Mossad**, not through the head of Tsomet.[^5]
- Its existence is **not acknowledged**: Ostrovsky writes that the Mossad “still doesn’t admit to the existence of Al.”[^6]

Ostrovsky writes that Al employs between 24 and 27 veteran field personnel, three of them active katsas; the book gives no date for the figure.[^7]

## Primary Known Operation: Andrew Young Affair (1979)

In Ostrovsky’s account, the Andrew Young affair is an example of alleged Al activity.[^8] He attributes the story to an informant and describes interception of a meeting involving Young, Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) observer Zehdi Terzi and Kuwait’s United Nations (UN) ambassador.[^8] The alleged operative’s name is withheld because no independent primary record authenticating his role is supplied here; the sequence below summarizes the memoir’s account.[^8]

**Operational sequence:**
1. Ostrovsky alleges that an Al team recorded the Young–Terzi meeting.[^9]
2. Ostrovsky alleges that an operative brought a transcript to Israel and accompanied Hofi to meet Begin; the operative’s identity is withheld.[^10]
3. Ostrovsky writes that Begin "wasn't interested in diplomacy" and "wanted blood."[^11]
4. Ostrovsky writes that **<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Yitzhak Hofi</span> opposed a press leak** and preferred private diplomacy, while Begin insisted on disclosure.[^12]
5. According to Ostrovsky, **Newsweek** was told that Young and Terzi had met; the transcript’s contents were withheld to protect the source.[^13]
6. Ostrovsky writes that Israel's UN ambassador **Yehuda Blum**, holding the transcript, drew from Young admissions going beyond what Young had told the State Department.[^14]
7. Andrew Young resigned on August 15, 1979.[^15]
8. Ostrovsky describes a subsequent crisis in **Black–Jewish relations** in the United States.[^16]

Ostrovsky expressly attributes this operational story to an informant.[^17] It is a reported memoir account, not his firsthand observation or an authenticated record establishing the interception and covert chain.[^17]

## Bugging of Arab Ambassadors' Homes

Ostrovsky describes alleged electronic surveillance of the homes and offices of Arab ambassadors and leaders in New York and Washington, including the Young meeting.[^18] His account does not independently establish how widespread such activity was or authenticate the alleged interceptions.[^18]

## Historical Context and Source Limitations

The following points distinguish Ostrovsky’s account of alleged intelligence activity from conclusions the memoir alone cannot establish:

1. **Clandestine activity:** Ostrovsky describes operations outside embassy cover.[^19] The cited memoir does not establish specific U.S. authorization, coordination or surveillance-court findings.

2. **Political use:** Ostrovsky writes that letting the meeting proceed would help “get rid of Young” and credits Al with ending his career.[^20] The memoir does not independently establish the covert collection chain or prove that destroying Young’s career was the unit’s objective.[^20]

3. **Denial:** Ostrovsky reports Mossad personnel denying that they operated in the United States.[^21] Such a denial does not establish the absence of diplomatic or legal accountability mechanisms.

4. **Limits of comparison:** The alleged sequence of surveillance and disclosure is not by itself evidence of a broader blackmail program or of a connection to another operation.[^22]

## Evidentiary Scope

The memoir offers a reported account of alleged covert political activity, which Ostrovsky attributes to an informant rather than firsthand observation.[^23] It does not independently establish an institutional connection to Epstein’s network.

## Open Questions

- Does the Al unit still exist in its 1979–1985 form, or has it evolved into a different organizational structure?
- What other US political figures were subjects of Al surveillance during the Carter, Reagan, and subsequent administrations?
- Was the Beekman Place town house bugging a one-time operation or part of a systematic program to surveil all Arab ambassadors' New York residences?
- Has any US counterintelligence investigation ever formally documented or acted on the Al unit's activities?
- What is the relationship, if any, between the Al unit's US operations and the later activities of figures like <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Robert Maxwell</span> and the Epstein network — i.e., is Al the institutionalized version of the kind of US-soil leverage collection that other sources allege continued through different mechanisms?
