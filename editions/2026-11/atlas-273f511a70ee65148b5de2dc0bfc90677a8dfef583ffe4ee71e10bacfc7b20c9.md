# Hughes-Ryan Amendment

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The Hughes-Ryan Amendment (1974) — § 662 of the Foreign Assistance Act of 1961, added by § 32 of the Foreign Assistance Act of 1974, Pub. L. No. 93-559, 88 Stat. 1795, 1804, codified at 22 U.S.C. § 2422[^1] — was the first statutory restriction on the CIA's Covert Action operations,[^2] requiring a presidential finding for covert actions and reporting to Congress in a "timely fashion";[^3] it did not itself require the finding to be written.[^4]

## Overview

Sponsored by Senator Harold Hughes (D-IA) and Representative Leo Ryan (D-CA) at the end of the 1974 congressional session,[^5] the amendment responded to congressional concern over covert action, especially Chile;[^6] final congressional action came in December 1974, around the time of the December 22, 1974 press revelation of CIA domestic surveillance.[^7] Per [Johnson (1989)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2600495), it "represented a bold attempt to replace plausible denial with a clear pathway of accountability for covert action — one that led straight to the Oval Office. The law formally forbade all covert actions not approved by the president himself."[^8]

**Statutory text** (the operative clause): "[N]o funds appropriated under the authority of this or any other Act may be expended by or on behalf of the [CIA] for operations in foreign countries, other than activities intended solely for obtaining necessary intelligence, unless and until the President finds that each such operation is important to the national security of the United States and reports, in a timely fashion, a description and scope of such operation to the appropriate committees of the Congress."[^9]

## Historical significance

**The "finding" term-of-art genealogy.** From the verb "finds" came the term of art "finding" — the anticipated written document of approval bearing the President's signature.[^10]

**The 24-hour notification expectation.** Initially, Hughes-Ryan required notification to six committees (three House, three Senate).[^11] "[W]ithin twenty-four hours quickly came to be the expectation in Congress" for the "timely fashion" language (Johnson 1989).[^12] The 1980 [Intelligence Oversight Act](atlas-57fbb56c98455e69a6a52b3b6c5a4fff423bc09eb5ac43701cbee952f9162362.md) reduced the notified committees from eight (the original six plus the Senate and House intelligence committees created in 1976 and 1977) to two (the SSCI and HPSCI).[^13]

**Iran-Contra and the finding procedure.** The Reagan-era Iran-Contra Affair sidestepped the normal Covert Action decision procedures, including Hughes-Ryan findings, by routing operations through the NSC staff (a White House entity not normally classified as an intelligence agency) and by "privatizing" funding through foreign-government and private-citizen sources outside appropriated funds.[^14] Johnson (1989) analyzed both techniques, drawing on the 1987 Tower Commission and congressional Iran-Contra reports.[^15]

**The pre-1947 historical-continuity counter-thesis.** Per Carter (2000), interbranch conflict over covert action pre-dates Hughes-Ryan by 150+ years.[^16]

## Open Questions

- Did Hughes-Ryan’s appropriated-funds scope make privatization circumvention legally inevitable, or was that practice a deliberate exploitation of a statutory gap?
