# Boland Amendment

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** A series of congressional restrictions on Contra aid (1982–1986), of which the committees designate the FY1983, FY1985 and FY1986 provisions Boland I, II and III,[^1] and the subject of a constitutional dispute between congressional authority and presidential foreign affairs power.[^2]

## Overview

The "Boland Amendment" is not a single law but a series of restrictions sponsored by Representative Edward P. Boland (D-MA), Chairman of the House Intelligence Committee.[^3] The three restrictions are set out below.[^4]

**Boland I** (December 1982, §793 of the FY1983 Defense Appropriations Act,[^5] Pub. L. 97-377):[^6] Barred the CIA and the Department of Defense from using the Act's funds to give military equipment, training, advice or other support to any group or individual not part of a country's armed forces "for the purpose of overthrowing the government of Nicaragua or provoking a military exchange between Nicaragua and Honduras."[^7] Limited in scope — it barred support given for the purpose of overthrowing the Nicaraguan government or provoking a Nicaragua–Honduras military exchange, and the Administration read it to turn on U.S. purpose rather than the Contras' intent.[^8]

**FY1984 funding cap** (December 1983,[^9] Pub. L. 98-212[^10] and Pub. L. 98-215):[^11] Limited Contra support to $24 million for fiscal year 1984.[^12]

**Boland II** (October 1984,[^13] §8066 of the FY1985 continuing appropriations act, Pub. L. 98-473):[^14] The operative version.[^15] Barred funds available to the CIA, the Department of Defense, or any other agency or entity "involved in intelligence activities" from being spent "for the purpose or which would have the effect of supporting, directly or indirectly, military or paramilitary operations in Nicaragua by any nation, group, organization, movement, or individual."[^16] This remained in effect from October 1984 through December 1985.[^17] The <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span> Committees found that both the letter and spirit of Boland II were violated.[^18]

**Clark Amendment** (1976): An earlier statutory restriction on assistance for military or paramilitary operations in Angola[^19] — cited by the Minority as evidence that Congress knew how to restrict presidential covert action when it chose to (and that its failure to do so for NSC staff was intentional).[^20]

## The NSC Exclusion Dispute

The Majority and Minority reports disagree on whether Boland II covered the National Security Council (NSC) staff:[^21][^25]

**Majority position**: Boland II covered the NSC Staff.[^21] The catch-all phrase "any other agency or entity . . . involved in intelligence activities" was descriptive, not a term of art, and reached the NSC staff once it ran a covert operation.[^22] The Sciaroni opinion (by the counsel to the President's Intelligence Oversight Board, September 1985)[^23] finding otherwise was flawed — Sciaroni had never practiced law before becoming the Board's counsel, the opinion was his first on legislation, and it rested on the incorrect factual premise that the NSC was "a coordinating body with no operational role."[^24]

**Minority position**: The NSC was **deliberately excluded** from the Intelligence Oversight Act of 1980[^25] (Harris's own 1980 proposal to include NSC was rejected during the drafting of the Intelligence Oversight Act).[^26] Draft C, an SSCI staff draft of a streamlined National Intelligence Act, would have confined special activities to the CIA unless the President determined that another agency should support an activity — it was not enacted.[^27] EO 12333 codified the NSC exclusion.[^28] The CIA legal memorandum distinguished participation, planning and providing advice for paramilitary operations (not permitted) from information-sharing, including advice on the delivery of supplies (permitted).[^29] The Minority argued that "virtually all, if not all, of Col. North's activities in support of the democratic resistance would have been legal even if the Boland Amendment had applied to the NSC."[^30]

**Minority constitutional argument (<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Cheney</span> et al.)**: Even if Boland textually covered the NSC, an appropriations restriction cannot abridge the President's Article II authority to conduct foreign policy.[^31] Where Congress cannot restrict a presidential action directly, the Minority argued, it cannot stop the President's staff from carrying it out; the Minority conceded that Congress could bar all U.S. Government spending on support for the Contras.[^32] This position — that even in Justice Jackson's "lowest ebb" category, whose framework the Supreme Court cited with approval in *Dames & Moore v. Regan*, 453 U.S. 654 (1981),[^33] Congress is "disabled" from interfering with some presidential foreign affairs matters[^34] — is an argument of the Cheney-led minority.

## Legislative History (Appendix A, William R. Harris)

The key primary source on the legislative history of the intelligence-oversight "entity" language is **Appendix A** of the Minority Report (Harris states that he does not know the specific legislative history of the Boland Amendments), authored by William R. Harris (longest continuously-serving SSCI consultant 1976–1984):[^35]

- NSC was **intentionally excluded** from IOA 1980 reporting requirements[^36]
- Harris's own 1980 proposal to include NSC was explicitly rejected[^37]
- Draft C (March 19, 1980) would have required special activities to be conducted only by the CIA, except when the President determined that another agency should support an activity — not enacted[^38]
- EO 12333 codified the NSC exclusion[^39]
- Harris's conclusion: presumption is NSC excluded, rebuttable by specific legislative history of particular Boland provisions — but the *default* is exclusion[^40]

## The Sciaroni Opinion

Intelligence Oversight Board counsel Bretton Sciaroni issued a September 1985 opinion finding Boland did not apply to the NSC.[^41] The Majority's criticisms of this opinion:[^42][^43]

- Had never practiced law before becoming the Board's counsel; the opinion was his first on legislation[^42]
- Rested on factual premises that turned out to be incorrect, including that the NSC was "a coordinating body with no operational role"[^43]

## The Diversion and Boland

The Majority argues the diversion itself violated Boland II and III because the proceeds came from selling United States weapons and so were United States funds.[^44] The CIA bought TOW missiles from the Department of Defense and delivered them to Iran through the Enterprise, acting as the CIA's agent.[^45] Because the proceeds were United States funds, the Majority found the diversion a violation of Boland II and III whatever the NSC's status.[^46]

## Open Questions

- Did Congress legislate a resolution of the NSC-exclusion dispute after <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span>, and how would that resolution apply to a later foreign-affairs funding restriction?
- Has a federal court reached the merits of an appropriations-versus-Article-II dispute in a context that could bear by analogy on the Boland dispute?
- Did the Boland Amendments as a whole bar U.S. government funds broadly for Contra support, and were the Iran-Contra operations as a whole, including the Iran arms sales, designed to circumvent them?
- Given the Majority’s conclusion that the diversion violated Boland regardless of how narrowly the statute was construed,[^46] how did the Minority address the diversion separately from its NSC-exclusion argument?
- Does the record itself characterize the Minority’s Article II/lowest-ebb argument as the core of its position?
- Given the restrictions enacted through Pub. L. 98-215 and the Intelligence Authorization Act containing Boland III,[^11][^17] what distinct role did appropriations and authorization acts play across the Boland restrictions?
- Given Harris’s account of the rejected 1980 NSC proposals and his rebuttable presumption for later Acts,[^37][^40] what specific legislative history would establish whether Boland II excluded the NSC?
