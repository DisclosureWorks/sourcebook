# Independent Counsel Statute (Ethics in Government Act)

Edition label: `2026-11`

**Definition:** Title VI of the Ethics in Government Act of 1978 (28 U.S.C. §§ 591–599), establishing the independent counsel mechanism[^1]; the statutory framework under which <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Lawrence Walsh</span> was appointed and operated[^2]; upheld by the Supreme Court in *Morrison v. Olson* (1988)[^3]

## Overview

The independent counsel statute was enacted after Watergate — a direct response to the Saturday Night Massacre and the perceived inability of the executive branch to investigate itself.[^4] The statute provided for appointment of an independent counsel by a special division of the D.C. Circuit upon application by the AG, with jurisdiction defined by the appointing court.[^5] The independent counsel could be removed only for good cause — not at presidential pleasure.[^21]

Walsh described the mechanism as a response to the structural conflict-of-interest problem he called the "irreconcilable conflict": the AG is both the President's chief legal adviser and the nation's top law enforcement officer.[^6] When the President or his senior advisers are under investigation, the AG cannot serve both roles impartially.[^7]

## Walsh's Appointment

Walsh was appointed Independent Counsel for Iran/Contra Matters on December 19, 1986.[^8] The appointing court defined his jurisdiction to cover both the Iran and Contra sides of the <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span> affair, including the diversion of arms-sale proceeds.[^9]

## Morrison v. Olson — Constitutional Challenge

The Supreme Court upheld the independent counsel statute 7–1 in *Morrison v. Olson*, a case arising from the appointment of Alexia Morrison to investigate Theodore Olson.[^10] The June 29, 1988 decision rejected the Separation of Powers challenge;[^11] Justice Scalia dissented, arguing that the statute impermissibly fragmented executive power.[^22]

The *Morrison v. Olson* decision removed the constitutional cloud over Walsh's court appointment.[^12] Walsh did not depend on the statute alone: he also held a back-up appointment from the attorney general, which a district court upheld in July 1987, allowing the grand jury investigation to continue while the statute stood struck down on appeal.[^13]

## The AG Conflict Problem — Walsh's Finding

Walsh's report identified two specific conflicts in the AG's role in independent counsel cases:[^14]

1. **The "irreconcilable conflict"**: The AG's roles as presidential adviser and as chief law enforcement officer create an irreconcilable conflict of interest.[^15] <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Meese</span>'s handling of the November 1986 "inquiry" demonstrated this: he held private conversations without taking notes and, even after learning of the diversion, failed to secure records in NSC staff offices.[^16]

2. **CIPA §6(e)**: The AG retains the authority to refuse to declassify classified information even in an independent counsel case — meaning the AG can block almost any potentially embarrassing prosecution that requires the declassification of information.[^17] Thornburgh used this authority to end the <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Fernandez</span> case and force dismissal of North's Count One.[^18]

Walsh suggested that the attorney general implement standards permitting independent review of a decision to block a prosecution, and argued that the AG's unrestricted §6(e) authority 'becomes questionable' when an independent counsel has jurisdiction.[^19]

## The Special Counsel Regulations

The Special Counsel regulations (28 C.F.R. Part 600) place appointment authority in the Attorney General, or the Acting Attorney General when the Attorney General is recused.[^23]
