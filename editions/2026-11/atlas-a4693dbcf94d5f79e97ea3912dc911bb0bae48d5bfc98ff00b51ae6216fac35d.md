**Definition:** A unit described by Victor Ostrovsky as responsible for assassinations within Mossad’s special-operations structure.[^1] The organizational descriptions, execution procedures and rules below summarize historical accounts by Ostrovsky and by Gordon Thomas and Martin Dillon; they are not independently authenticated directives, legal findings or a verified description of present operations.[^1][^24][^13]

## Overview

In *By Way of Deception*, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Victor Ostrovsky</span> describes *kidon*, translated as “bayonet,” as a unit within *Komemiute*, formerly *Metsada*.[^2] His **1990 book already uses the name Komemiute**; the organizational description and execution-list account are historical claims in that book.[^3][^4][^5]

Ostrovsky writes that he was approached in 1979 and turned the job down because it would keep him away from his family, and that he learned only after joining the Mossad that he had been groomed for *kidon*.[^6] He re-entered the process in October 1982.[^6]

## Execution-List Process

Ostrovsky describes the following sequence for the formal execution list, which he distinguishes from “operational emergency” killings decided by the head of the Mossad:[^7]

1. **Request**: the head of the Mossad asks the Prime Minister's Office to place a name on the list[^7]
2. **PM referral**: the prime minister sends it to a special judicial committee[^7]
3. **Secret judicial committee**: a committee whose existence is unknown to the Israeli Supreme Court; it sits as a military court and is composed of intelligence, military, and justice officials; hearings are held at various locations, often a private residence; membership and location change for each case[^7]
4. **Trial in absentia**: Two lawyers assigned — prosecution and defense; accused is unaware of any proceedings[^7]
5. **Verdict**: Guilty → order to bring to Israel for trial, *or* execute at first opportunity[^7]
6. **PM signature**: the execution order requires the prime minister's signature; some sign in advance, others first consider whether the hit would create political difficulties at the time[^7]

Ostrovsky writes that the list varies from one or two names to about 100, depending on the extent of anti-Israeli terrorist activity.[^7]

Ostrovsky describes reviewing the execution list as **one of** a new Israeli prime minister’s first duties.[^8]

## Operation Sphinx Example

In the Osirak prologue of *By Way of Deception*, Ostrovsky attributes two 1980 killings in Paris to Mossad.[^9] This is the memoir’s allegation, without a contemporaneous police or judicial finding here.[^9] Victim-identifying details, the room number and graphic mechanisms are omitted.

## Meir Amit’s Assassination Rules, as Described by Gordon Thomas and Martin Dillon

Thomas and Dillon (*<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Robert Maxwell</span>, Israel's Superspy*) attribute to Mossad Director Meir Amit the following rules for assassination:[^10]

1. No killing of political leaders.[^10]
2. No killing of a terrorist's family unless directly implicated.[^10]
3. Each execution requires PM sanction.[^10]
4. Framing: "Any execution is therefore not state-sponsored murder but an ultimate judicial sanction by the state."[^11]

Thomas and Dillon present these as rules attributed to Meir Amit.[^10]

## CIA Assassination Manual (Gottlieb, 1953)

Gordon Thomas and Martin Dillon allege that an eight-page 1953 Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) assassination manual attributed to Sidney Gottlieb was used at the Mossad academy.[^12] Authorship, length and transfer have not been independently verified here; the passage alone does not establish a formal doctrinal exchange.

## Maxwell Case — September 1991 Kidon Meeting

Gordon Thomas and Martin Dillon allege that four men met to consider how to deal with Maxwell in September 1991.[^13][^14] Their account alleges an assassination plan,[^13] but supplies no authenticated meeting record here; identities are withheld because identification and necessity have not been established.

- Thomas and Dillon place the alleged first meeting at Mossad headquarters on **King Saul Boulevard**, Tel Aviv; participant names are withheld.[^15]
- Thomas and Dillon place an alleged **second meeting**, ten days after the first, at a Pinsker Street safe house; the headquarters and safe-house meetings are separate episodes in their account.[^16]
- Thomas and Dillon allege that Maxwell threatened to disclose intelligence activities unless he received financial help; this is their account of his motive and conduct.[^17]
- The authors portray the alleged discussions as concerned with the damage Maxwell’s disclosures could cause; the reconstructed conversation is paraphrased and the participant’s identity withheld.[^18]
- Thomas and Dillon frame the meetings as preparations to act against Maxwell.[^19] Their narrative does not independently establish an assassination decision or the cause of his death.[^19][^20]

Descriptions of technical capabilities in this narrative do not establish that such means were used against Maxwell. No independently authenticated operational record is supplied here; unnecessary laboratory and weapon details are omitted.
