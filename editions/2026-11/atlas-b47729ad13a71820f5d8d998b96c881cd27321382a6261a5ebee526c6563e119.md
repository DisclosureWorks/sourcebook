# Double Government / Trumanite Network

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** Michael <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Glennon</span>'s framework describing the bifurcation of U.S. governance into visible "Madisonian" institutions that provide constitutional legitimacy but exercise little real constraint[^1], and a concealed "Trumanite network" of several hundred senior national security officials that makes actual policy. Named by analogy to Walter Bagehot's 1867 analysis of British constitutional duality.[^2]

## Overview

Walter Bagehot's *The English Constitution* distinguishes the parts of government that command public reverence from the parts that actually govern.[^3] Bagehot argued that this duality was self-sustaining as long as the public believed the dignified institutions were in charge.[^4]

<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Michael Glennon</span> applied this framework to U.S. national security in a 2014 article in the *Harvard National Security Journal*.[^86]

The Trumanite network consists of several hundred senior executive officials who manage the military, intelligence, diplomatic, and law enforcement agencies responsible for national security.[^5] Glennon describes a network including careerists and political appointees.[^6] His comparison of 247 appointees with 668,000 civilian employees refers to the Department of Defense and related agencies in 2004.[^7]

## The Trumanite Network — Origins and Operation

**Origins:** The National Security Act of 1947 established the CIA[^8], the statutory Joint Chiefs of Staff[^9] and the National Security Council;[^10] the NSA was established separately by Truman’s 1952 directive.[^11] Harry Truman, who feared military overreach, nonetheless created the apparatus.[^12] Conservative Republicans at the time warned it would produce a "garrison state."[^13] Congress named the agency in a 1959 public law (Pub. L. 86-36)[^14], and it drew sustained public attention only in the 1970s, when the Church Committee disclosed its watch list of Vietnam War opponents.

**Scale:** Priest and Arkin's 2010 *Top Secret America* investigation identified 1,271 government organizations and 1,931 private companies working on counterterrorism, homeland security and intelligence in about 10,000 U.S. locations, and estimated that 854,000 people held top-secret clearances.[^15]

**Structural Incentive to Exaggerate Threats:** In Glennon's account, the network's structural feature is an asymmetric cost structure.[^16] Overprotection costs, such as disproportionate money or manpower devoted to security, are externalized: in Glennon's account, the President, Congress and a federal agency must budget for, vote for and collect the taxes that pay for them.[^16][^17] Underprotection costs (missing an attack, failing to prevent a threat) are internalized — Trumanites are blamed.[^17] Glennon argues that this creates a rational incentive to exaggerate threats, expand programs, and resist retrenchment.[^18] Glennon, citing Sanger, reports that in the decade after 9/11 the United States spent at least $3.3 trillion responding to the attacks, or $6.6 million for every dollar al-Qaeda spent to stage them.[^19]

**Secrecy Culture:** In Glennon's account, non-disclosure agreements, SCIF culture, over-classification, and aggressive whistleblower prosecution combine to create an information asymmetry that prevents meaningful oversight.[^20] Glennon cites Balkin's characterization of the network as comprising "information gluttons and information misers."[^20]

**Conformism:** The network's grundnorm is stability.[^21] For an ambitious Trumanite, Glennon describes proposing the continuation of existing policy as the safe course.[^22] <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Frank Church</span> described national security policy as a "self-generating enterprise."[^23] Dissenters risk marginalization.[^24]

## The Madisonian Institutions — Sources of Illusion

Five elements maintain the appearance of Madisonian control, even while the Madisonians exercise little real power: (1) historical pedigree (the visible institutions have deep constitutional roots);[^25] (2) solemn ritual (confirmation hearings, State of the Union, oral argument); (3) intelligibility (Madisonian proceedings are public and comprehensible);[^26] (4) mystery (Madisonian officials operate at a remove from the public, and what happens in their deliberations is "shrouded in mystery");[^27] (5) superficial harmony (Madisonian statements closely track Trumanite policies, creating appearance of alignment).[^28]

> "The Trumanite network makes American national security policy; it is occasional exceptions to that policy that are made by the Madisonian institutions."[^29] — Glennon

## Judicial Capture

Federal judiciary nominees in national security-adjacent roles are effectively pre-vetted by the apparatus whose cases they will later adjudicate.[^30] Glennon traces the careers of Rehnquist (OLC, military surveillance, *Laird v. Tatum* non-recusal), Scalia (OLC, covert operations), Alito (Army Signal Corps → OLC → presidential power positions), and Roberts (Rehnquist clerk, strong executive positions) as examples of capture through selection.[^31]

The FISC approved 32,087 of 32,093 warrant requests between 1979 and 2011 — a 99.98% approval rate.[^32] Chief Judge Walton acknowledged the FISC "is forced to rely upon the accuracy of the information that is provided to the Court."[^33] In 2011, Chief Judge Bates issued a declassified opinion finding that NSA's representations had constituted "the third instance in less than three years in which the government has disclosed a substantial misrepresentation regarding the scope of a major collection program."[^34] Bates’s 2011 footnote recounts the court’s March 2009 finding that the required metadata-querying standard, a critical element of the regime, had never functioned effectively.[^35]

Non-delegation doctrine is never enforced in national security;[^36] state secrets doctrine eliminates merits adjudication;[^37] standing doctrine barriers prevent suits from reaching the merits.[^38]

## Congressional Capture

Trumanites draft national security legislation, then present it to Congress.[^39] The legislative veto — the mechanism by which Congress could claw back delegated authority — was invalidated by *INS v. Chadha* (1983).[^40] The 9/11 Commission found oversight "dysfunctional."[^41] As Glennon quotes James Bamford, "the intelligence committees are more dedicated to protecting the agencies from budget cuts than safeguarding the public from their transgressions."[^42] Committee and chamber rules condition the sharing of classified information with other Members; access may be denied, partial, or full.[^43]

The Senate Intelligence Committee Chair, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Dianne Feinstein</span>, (1) confirmed she had no knowledge of how the NSA actually used the phone records it collected; (2) stated it was nonetheless important to collect records in case someone might become a terrorist in the future — a rationale the *New York Times* called "absurd".[^44]

Glennon writes that Clapper's March 2013 testimony to Wyden — "No, sir" to the question whether NSA collected data on millions of Americans — later admitted by Clapper to be "the least untruthful" statement he could give, was potentially a felony (18 U.S.C. § 1001).[^45]

When all Senators were invited to a classified briefing on NSA programs following the Snowden disclosures, fewer than half attended.[^46] The House Intelligence Committee Chair, Rep. Mike Rogers, stated in debate over restricting the bulk phone records collection program: "*we the intelligence community*—missed a huge clue."[^47] Glennon concludes that "the overseers and the overseen had, at length, become one";[^48] the Senate Intelligence Committee chair's March 2014 floor statement records a public challenge to CIA conduct (see [Feinstein, Dianne. “CIA Detention and Interrogation Report.” Senate floor statement. *Congressional Record* 160, no. 40 (March 11, 2014): S1487–S1491.](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CREC-2014-03-11/pdf/CREC-2014-03-11-pt1-PgS1487-8.pdf)).

## Presidential Capture

Trumanites formulate the options that presidents nominally choose.[^49] Glennon quotes Secretary Robert Gates calling this "the classic Henry Kissinger model" — three options presented, two of them ridiculous.[^50] Presidents discover that "could not" and "would not" shade into each other.[^51]

- **Afghanistan:** Glennon, citing Woodward, recounts that when Obama asked the eighteen top advisers at an Afghanistan-Pakistan strategy review meeting, "Is there anybody who thinks we ought to leave Afghanistan?", no one said anything.[^52]
- **Drone policy:** Continued unanimously by CIA, DNI, Pentagon, and Brennan.[^53]
- **<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Robert Gates</span> case study:** CIA 1965 → NSC under Nixon/Ford → <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Casey</span> chief of staff (1981) → <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span> blocked nomination → returned to the White House in January 1989 as Deputy Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs → confirmed as CIA DCI 1991 → SecDef under <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">George W. Bush</span> (2006–2009) → SecDef under Obama (2009–2011).[^54] Gates served both administrations without interruption; Glennon presents his staying power as evidence of the Trumanite network's enduring grip.[^55]

## NSA Case Study

- 1952: NSA established by a Top Secret directive issued by President Truman[^56]
- 1975: Church Committee disclosed NSA watch-list and SHAMROCK activity; Senator Hart said SHAMROCK appeared unlawful under section 605 and the Fourth Amendment, while noting no case exactly in point.[^57]
- 1978: FISA enacted as post-Church reform; created FISC[^58]
- Pre-9/11: NSA Director <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Hayden</span> proposed further expansion[^59]
- October 4, 2001: President George W. Bush issued a Top Secret authorization directing that NSA signals-intelligence capabilities be used to detect and prevent further attacks inside the United States (the start of the President's Surveillance Program).[^60]
- March 10, 2004: at a hospital-room meeting attended by <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Comey</span> and Goldsmith, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Ashcroft</span>, according to Comey's Senate testimony as recounted in the Inspectors General's report, told White House officials of his objections to the program and said he was not the Attorney General, indicating Comey.[^87]
- July 14, 2004: the FISC signed the first pen-register/trap-and-trace order, giving NSA essentially the same authority to collect bulk Internet metadata it had had under the President's Surveillance Program;[^61] other program activities moved under FISA court orders in stages through January 2007.[^62]
- December 16, 2005: NYT disclosed warrantless surveillance (held >1 year at Bush administration request)[^63]
- 2008: FISA Amendments Act, §702[^64]; PRISM program authorized[^65]; Obama continued[^66]
- March 2013: Clapper gives testimony to Feinstein's committee that he later acknowledges was erroneous[^67]
- June 2013: Snowden disclosures[^68]
- July 2013: House defeated Amash-Conyers amendment to terminate bulk collection 205-217; Obama administration allied with House Republican leadership to defeat it[^69]

## Future Risk Scenarios

Glennon identifies two possible trajectories if Bagehot's "cone of governance" tips:[^88]

1. **Gradual**: Madisonian institutions fade into museum pieces like the British House of Lords; Madisonians "kiss babies, cut ribbons, and read Trumanite talking points"; the Trumanite network "takes on the substance of a silent directorate."[^70]

2. **Catastrophic**: A major terrorist attack occurs in the absence of Madisonian safety-valve checks.[^71] Initial "rally round the flag" fervor is followed by escalating mutual recrimination, criminalization of dissent, and governmental surveillance of the public.[^72] Historical precedents cited: Alien and Sedition Acts (1790s), Palmer Raids (1919–20), Japanese-American internment (1940s), governmental spying on and disruption of civil rights, draft protesters, and anti-war activists (1960s–70s), post-9/11 "unlawful combatant" incommunicado detention.[^73]

**The negative feedback loop:** Resuscitating Madisonian institutions requires an informed, engaged electorate.[^74] But voters have rational reasons not to become informed when national security policy persists unchanged regardless of electoral outcomes.[^75] As the public disengages, Madisonian institutions atrophy further, accelerating the cycle.[^76]

## Intellectual Antecedents

Glennon situates his framework within a larger body of thought on bureaucracy, power, and democratic accountability:[^89]

- **Max Weber:** bureaucratization can lead to "polar night of icy darkness" where humanitarian values are sacrificed for abstract organizational ends[^77]
- **Friedrich Hayek** (as quoted by Glennon): "the greatest danger to liberty today comes from the men who are most needed and most powerful in government, namely, the efficient expert administrators"[^78]
- **Erich Fromm:** people unconsciously adopt societal norms to "escape from freedom"[^79]
- **Irving Janis:** high esprit de corps increases groupthink risk; "irrational and dehumanizing actions directed against out-groups"[^80]
- **C. Wright Mills:** *The Power Elite* (1956); centralization among officials sharing a worldview who operate in secrecy → "military metaphysics" and "permanent war economy"[^81]
- **Malcolm Moos / Eisenhower:** Moos, Eisenhower's speechwriter, was familiar with Mills's work; Eisenhower's Farewell Address (January 17, 1961) warned of "unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex"[^82]
