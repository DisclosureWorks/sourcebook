# Right to Be Let Alone

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** Justice Brandeis's articulation in his *Olmstead* (1928) dissent of the most fundamental privacy right protected by the Constitution — "the most comprehensive of rights and the right most valued by civilized men."[^1]

## Overview

Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis used and developed the phrase in their 1890 *Harvard Law Review* article “The Right to Privacy” (4 Harv. L. Rev. 193), while crediting Judge Thomas Cooley with the earlier formulation of a right “to be let alone”.[^2] Brandeis carried the formulation into his *Olmstead* dissent (277 U.S. 438, 478) thirty-eight years later, where he framed it as a constitutional principle protecting against government intrusion.[^3]

The formulation was a dissenting position in 1928, and later opinions of the Court have quoted it.[^7][^8] *Katz v. United States* (1967) overruled *Olmstead*'s trespass doctrine,[^4] and the phrase appears in later privacy decisions including *Stanley v. Georgia* (1969)[^7] and *Eisenstadt v. Baird* (1972).[^8]

## Later influence

A line of development runs from *Olmstead* (1928) through *Katz* (1967) and *Keith* (1972), which to some degree presaged Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) (1978); the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board (PCLOB)'s 2014 Section 215 report traces Fourth Amendment doctrine from *Olmstead* to *Katz*.[^9] The Wyden-Udall letters (2011–12) raised the related problem of secret interpretations of surveillance law.[^5] The Church Committee Book II (1976) quoted Brandeis's *Olmstead* dissent on surveillance technology in its National Security Agency (NSA) findings, and treated mail opening (HTLINGUAL) and Counterintelligence Program (COINTELPRO) as matters of constitutional as well as statutory violation; any reliance on the Right to Be Let Alone framework is implicit.[^6]

## Open Questions

- **Question:** How does Book II’s discussion of surveillance technology, mail opening (HTLINGUAL), and COINTELPRO relate to Brandeis’s Right to Be Let Alone framework?
