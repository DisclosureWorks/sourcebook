# "Rogue Agency" Narrative

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The Ford-era executive-branch analytic frame that characterized 1960s–early-1970s Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) abuses (CHAOS, mail-opening, surveillance of journalists and dissidents, foreign assassination plots) as **isolated incidents that occurred without presidential authorization** rather than as policies authorized by Presidents Eisenhower, Kennedy, Johnson, or Nixon.[^1] Per Kitts (1996), the framing was deliberately strategic: by presenting the abuses this way — the "rogue agency" argument, in Kitts's phrase — the Ford administration could argue the appropriate remedy was internal executive-branch reform rather than expanded congressional oversight.[^2]

## Overview

1. **The <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Rockefeller Commission</span> mandate** (Executive Order (EO) 11828, January 4, 1975) limited investigation to domestic CIA activity, with (in Kitts's phrase) "technical compliance with the law" as the yardstick.[^3]
2. **The Rockefeller Commission's final report** (June 6, 1975) found that "the great majority of the CIA's domestic activities comply with its statutory authority," while some activities fell within a "doubtful area" and some "were plainly unlawful"; it also said that some "were initiated or ordered by Presidents, either directly or indirectly."[^4] The suppressed assassination chapter would have complicated the rogue-agency framing.[^5] The Church Committee later said the evidence permitted "a reasonable inference that the plot to assassinate Lumumba was authorized by President Eisenhower," but made no finding because of countervailing testimony; it found "insufficient evidence" that Presidents Eisenhower, Kennedy, or Johnson authorized the Castro plots.[^6] Kitts concludes that White House pressure was "the crucial factor in killing that part of the report": Belin recalled that "the State Department" — "that meant Kissinger" — felt it would be "inappropriate for a presidentially appointed commission" to report CIA involvement in assassination plans, and the commissioners later voted not to include the section.[^7]
3. **EO 11905** (February 18, 1976)[^8] created three new oversight bodies all internal to the executive branch (Committee on Foreign Intelligence under National Security Council (NSC); Operations Advisory Group; Intelligence Oversight Board); broadened Director of Central Intelligence (DCI) authority; and, separately from the order, a Ford proposal to *reduce* the number of legislators involved in intelligence oversight via a joint intelligence oversight committee. Kitts describes the "flip side" of the framing: if the abuses were isolated incidents, there was no need for an expanded congressional role.[^9]

## Historical significance

Per Kitts (1996) (p. 1093, immediately before the Conclusion):[^10]

> "Ford had consistently attempted to portray the intelligence abuses as isolated incidents that had occurred without presidential authorization. In large part, the report of the Rockefeller Commission adopted this same point of view, thus giving Ford's argument something of an official basis. The flip side of this contention was, of course, that there was no need for an expanded congressional role in the process of intelligence oversight."[^10][^11][^12]

> "While it was true that some of the questionable activities had indeed been isolated, it was equally true that some had been undertaken at the direction of past presidents. Nowhere was this more apparent than in the case of the foreign assassination plots. For this reason, Ford was determined to avoid the inclusion of that issue in the commission's final report. The assassination chapter-that-never-was would have weakened the 'rogue agency' argument, and thus pointed to the need for greater congressional supervision of executive branch intelligence activities."[^13][^14]

Kitts (1996) argues that the rogue-agency framing supported Ford's opposition to an expanded congressional role, while evidence of presidentially directed abuses pointed toward greater congressional supervision.[^15]

**The Church Committee's challenge to the framing.** During Robert McNamara's July 11, 1975 testimony, committee chairman <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Frank Church</span> set the rogue-agency explanation beside its alternatives rather than adopting it: "Either the CIA was a rogue elephant rampaging out of control, over which no effective direction was being given in this matter of assassination, or there was some secret channel circumventing the whole structure of command" through which the plots had been authorized; his third possibility was that the officials involved had "misinterpreted their scope of authority" ([Church Committee assassination report (1975)](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/sites-default-files-94465.pdf), p. 158).[^16] The Church Committee reported operation-specific evidence and emphasized that accountability arrangements permitted assassination plots without clear express authorization; it did not find uniform presidential authorization across administrations.[^17] The Ford administration's counter-mobilization later shifted from supporting the Rockefeller Commission's containment role to handling the administration's responses to the Church/Pike investigations (Sept 1975 – Feb 1976 via the [Intelligence Coordinating Group (ICG)](atlas-d151e008a67cac21e1402ad8a19dc3fbc25ff7f39c3c310975e615604d3afa41.md)); Olmsted attributes that shift to the congressional inquiries' momentum in fall 1975 and to lessons from the Rockefeller episode.[^18]

**Modern recurrences.** Applying the rogue-agency framework to post-9/11 torture, post-Snowden surveillance, or rendition abuses requires episode-specific post-2001 evidence that the cited 1996 sources do not supply.

## Open Questions

- How does the rogue-agency narrative compare with modern "deep state"[^19] rhetoric, and under what circumstances does each frame assign responsibility to presidents or agencies?
- Is "MHCHAOS"[^20] distinct from Operation CHAOS or an alternate name for the same program?
- Is treating the Rockefeller Commission's mandate, final report and EO 11905 as three vehicles of the narrative a scholarly framework or an analytical grouping?
