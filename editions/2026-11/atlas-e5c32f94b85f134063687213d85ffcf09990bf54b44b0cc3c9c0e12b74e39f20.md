# Declassification Monopoly

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** This page's label for the asymmetry Olmsted (1996) describes: the executive branch's unilateral authority to declassify and disclose classified information for political advantage, together with its de facto power to characterize congressional disclosure as illegitimate "leaking".[^1]

## Overview

The asymmetry has two components:

1. **Unilateral declassification:** Olmsted (1996) writes, "If the president wanted information disclosed, he could simply reveal it. The executive branch could declassify at will."[^2] (Olmsted 1996).[^3]

2. **De facto delegitimization of congressional disclosure:** When Congress publishes sensitive material — even material already widely reported in the press — the executive branch can characterize this as "leaking" or "endangering national security."[^4] Olmsted documents how <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Colby</span>'s November 1975 criticism of the Church Committee for publishing previously-reported names in its assassination report was used to imply the committee was endangering "human lives"[^5] — even though one of the named individuals had identified himself in a letter to the editor and Church Committee member <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Barry Goldwater</span> (one of the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)'s strongest supporters) agreed the names should be published.[^6]

In Olmsted's account, the combination of these two — selective disclosure when it serves the executive's narrative, plus delegitimization of any congressional symmetric disclosure — produces an asymmetric information environment in which the executive branch can shape the public debate while constraining Congress's ability to rebut.[^7]

## Historical significance

In Olmsted's account, the president's monopoly on authorized disclosure was the most important weapon in the Ford White House's public-relations campaign, whose responses to the committees the [Intelligence Coordinating Group (ICG)](atlas-d151e008a67cac21e1402ad8a19dc3fbc25ff7f39c3c310975e615604d3afa41.md) coordinated:[^8]

- Cheney's December 27, 1974 advice to Ford, recorded in a draft memo (Olmsted) or meeting notes (Kitts), recommended a presidential "mechanism to investigate" whose goals included heading off "Congressional efforts to further encroach on the executive branch."[^9]
- The [Welch operation](atlas-66cdd8443ab6cb5a8313b378422f4b1137d81259c4061ec94a815378b253978d.md) (Dec 1975 – Jan 1976) drew on the executive branch's command of media attention: the White House staged the return and burial while leaving the blame for the killing implied rather than stated.[^10]
- The [Pike Report suppression](atlas-c3d52c8c6f8c310282d1e2a875c77e973f3b5d9dc42c95b0774b156429630a38.md) (January 1976) was driven by the executive branch's ability to reframe Pike's refusal to let the executive censor the final report as irresponsible "leaking"; in October 1975 Pike had accepted a procedure referring disputed disclosures to the President.[^11]
- Ford's Feb 1976 reform proposals included **criminal penalties for government employees' unauthorized disclosures of intelligence "sources and methods"** — including disclosures to journalists.[^12]
