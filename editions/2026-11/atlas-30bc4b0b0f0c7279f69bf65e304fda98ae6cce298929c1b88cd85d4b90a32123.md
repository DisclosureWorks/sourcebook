# Jordan Destabilization Doctrine

**Definition:** In his 1994 memoir, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Victor Ostrovsky</span> recounts an interlocutor’s allegation of a Mossad faction’s plan to destabilize Jordan through counterfeit currency, arming fundamentalists and killings intended to provoke unrest.[^1] These are the memoir’s alleged components, not an independently established institutional doctrine or directive.[^1]

## Source

[Ostrovsky, Victor. *The Other Side of Deception: A Rogue Agent Exposes the Mossad’s Secret Agenda*. First edition. New York: HarperCollins, 1994. ISBN 0-06-017635-0. Held electronic version.](https://archive.org/details/othersideofdecep00ostr), Chapter 18, PDF pp. 132–140: Ostrovsky’s narrative places the conversation in Washington before his May 1986 Jordan trip, which he dates on PDF p. 153, not at the later Ottawa meeting in Chapter 22.[^2] The quotation below is reported speech in the memoir, not an authenticated transcript:[^3]

> *"They've decided that it's time to destabilize Jordan to the point of civil anarchy."*[^3]

## The three operational components

The following alleged plan and quotations are Ostrovsky’s reported conversation.[^4] Neither the dialogue, the first-name speech tag below nor the projected effects independently authenticate a participant or an implemented operation:[^4]

1. **Counterfeit currency, in the reported plan.**[^5] *"A high influx of counterfeit currency, causing distrust in the market."*[^5]

2. **Arming fundamentalist groups:** Ostrovsky’s reported dialogue describes supplying groups compared with Hamas and the Muslim Brotherhood.[^6] This is retrospective wording in his 1994 memoir, not evidence of an implemented 1986 program or a contemporary directive naming Hamas.[^23]

3. **Killing figures described as symbols of stability, in the reported plan.**[^7] *"Assassinating leading figures who are symbols of stability, causing riots in the universities and forcing the government to respond with harsh measures and lose popularity."*[^7]

Ephraim to Ostrovsky: *"You know what I'm talking about; everything you learned about and more."*[^8]

## The Egypt parallel

In the same reported conversation, Ostrovsky’s interlocutor also alleges a planned effort against Egypt; the passage does not establish that the plan was implemented:[^9]

Ostrovsky reports that his interlocutor said he knew of a similar plan for Egypt and claimed involvement in it, with time remaining before implementation.[^10] This is an unquoted paraphrase of the memoir’s reported speech, retaining the speaker’s qualification.[^10]

A later passage alleges an arms route to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood through Sinai couriers and an arms-for-targets arrangement (see [Hamas-for-PLO doctrine](atlas-0e373e0aeb8cea9b1c722070913ac8e67973bc119cf2700b6e6b79d16fef955e.md)).[^11] Its connection to the earlier alleged plan is an interpretation within one memoir, not independent evidence of implementation or a particular assassination.[^3][^11]

## The doctrinal frame: peace-accord-sabotage

Ostrovsky relates these alleged plans to opposition to peace initiatives.[^12] The examples below are accounts in his memoir, not established operations or proof of an institutional policy:[^12]

- Ostrovsky reports an allegation that a team undermined a Jordanian peace initiative.[^13] The identity-unresolved alleged operative is omitted.[^13]
- Ostrovsky reports his interlocutor’s allegation that the 1982 killings of Ray and Barsimantov followed a leak of their effort to open a dialogue with the Palestinians; the same account says the killers did not know who the target was and that the killing was later said to be a mistake.[^14] The memoir does not independently establish the covert responsibility or motive.[^14]
- Ostrovsky attributes the complaint about leaked diplomatic meetings to an unnamed Jordanian embassy interviewer, before that interviewer introduces his superior; it is not the superior’s statement.[^15]

## Ostrovsky's counter-role

Ostrovsky says he was sent to warn the Jordanians of the alleged plan and that Jordan’s military attaché in Washington facilitated his May 1986 visit.[^16][^17] He expressly distinguishes the attaché from the intelligence apparatus.[^17] This is Ostrovsky’s autobiographical account, without a primary operational record here.[^16][^17]

## Significance

The following comparison concerns three methods alleged in Ostrovsky’s memoir; it does not establish their implementation:[^18]

1. **Counterfeit currency**, an alleged method in the memoir[^18]
2. **Arming fundamentalist groups**[^18]
3. **Killing figures described as symbols of stability**, which the reported plan links to riots, a harsh government response and loss of popularity[^18]

On this page’s reading: Similar themes recur in Ostrovsky’s accounts, including <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Operation Brush-Fire</span>.[^24] That comparison within one author’s work supplies no independent corroboration of an institutional doctrine or particular killings.
