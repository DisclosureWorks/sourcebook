# "Invitation to Struggle"

Edition label: `2026-11`

**Definition:** <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Edward S. Corwin</span>'s mid-20th-century formulation of the relationship between the President and Congress on foreign policy and national security, set out in *The President: Office and Powers* (1940; 5th rev. ed. 1984).[^1][^2] The formulation[^2]:

> "[T]he Constitution, considered only for its affirmative grants of powers capable of affecting the issue, is an invitation to struggle for the privilege of directing American foreign policy."[^3]

— Corwin et al., *The President: Office and Powers, 1787-1984* (5th rev. ed. 1984), p. 201.[^4]

## Overview

Corwin's formulation establishes that the U.S. constitutional architecture **does not pre-determine** which branch should have substantive authority over foreign policy and national security; instead, the architecture creates a structural incentive for inter-branch conflict that is resolved (when resolved at all) through political and institutional processes rather than through clear constitutional text.[^5] On this page's reading, the "invitation to struggle" is descriptive — it does not prescribe an outcome, only a structural feature of the inter-branch relationship.

Three later works this page reads alongside the formulation:

- [Bruemmer (1992)](https://openyls.law.yale.edu/entities/publication/f24d637a-5286-41ea-8701-e0ca63f4bc44) adopts Corwin's formulation as the framing for his argument that both President and Congress should decline the "invitation to struggle" and proceed by cooperative reorganization (President proposes; Congress approves up-or-down).[^6]
- [Harold Koh, *The National Security Constitution* (1990)](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300044935/the-national-security-constitution/) is cited here only through other authors, because Parallax's copy is encrypted and cannot be opened and the book has not been read directly: [Bruemmer (1992)](https://openyls.law.yale.edu/entities/publication/f24d637a-5286-41ea-8701-e0ca63f4bc44) reports that Koh offers "fast-track approval" as a model for foreign affairs legislation, and Olmsted (1996) reports Koh's view that the Iran-Contra scandal demonstrates that the "deeper systemic flaws" exposed by the intelligence inquiries, as well as by Vietnam and Watergate, were never remedied.[^7]
- [Glennon, *Double Government* (2014)](https://harvardnsj.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2014/01/Glennon-Final.pdf) expressly invokes Corwin, arguing that the apparent balance among the branches is not the outcome of the struggle Corwin described: all three Madisonian institutions (the courts, Congress and the presidency) have lost effective authority to the Trumanite bureaucratic network.[^8]

## Analytical significance

Bruemmer, a former Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) General Counsel writing in private practice and expressing his own views, recommends cooperative procedures using the Defense Base Closure model while favoring presidential leadership.[^10]

Would such a revision change the predicted outcomes of executive counter-mobilization?

- Given Bruemmer's preference for presidential leadership and a secondary congressional role,[^10] how does his Defense Base Closure proposal relate to executive counter-mobilization?
- Does executive-branch conduct following Bruemmer's 1992 proposal show that structural pressures override individual reform preferences, and what evidence would distinguish that explanation from competing accounts?
- Did the first edition of Corwin's *The President: Office and Powers* already contain the "invitation to struggle" passage?
