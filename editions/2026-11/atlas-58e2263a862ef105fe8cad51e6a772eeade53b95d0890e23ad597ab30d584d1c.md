# The Iran-Contra Diversion

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Oliver North</span>'s scheme to use the financial surplus from overpriced Iranian arms sales to fund the Contra program[^1]; documented in North's early-April 1986 memorandum to <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Poindexter</span>[^2]; at the center of the <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span> scandal when <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Meese</span> announced it on November 25, 1986[^3]

## Overview

The diversion was the financial mechanism that linked the two covert operations at the center of the <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span> affair.[^4] North structured arms sales to Iran at prices far above the U.S. government's cost: approximately $10,000 per TOW missile against approximately $3,700 charged per TOW including handling.[^5] The surplus accumulated in Lake Resources (a Panamanian shell corporation with a Swiss bank account managed by <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Willard Zucker</span> on behalf of the Enterprise).[^6] North proposed directing a portion of this surplus to the Contras[^7] — at the time subject to Boland restrictions on assistance to the Contra military effort, with specified nonlethal exceptions.[^8]

## Origin

North testified that Ghorbanifar suggested Contra funding during a January 1986 London bathroom conversation; the reports also document earlier diversion planning and dispute the origin account.[^9] North developed a concrete plan to divert arms-sale proceeds to the Contras.[^10] His early-April 1986 memorandum to Poindexter, titled "Release of American Hostages in Beirut,"[^11] allocated the anticipated proceeds: $2M for replacement TOWs (reimbursing Israel for the 1985 shipments)[^12], **$12M for the Nicaraguan Democratic Resistance Forces**.[^13]

Poindexter approved the diversion orally, on what he described as his own authority[^14] — he testified he deliberately did not inform Reagan in order to preserve "plausible deniability."[^15] Walsh reported no direct evidence that Reagan authorized or was informed of the diversion, while expressing doubt that Reagan lacked knowledge.[^16]

## Financial Structure

- **Total Enterprise income**: more than $47.6M[^17]
- **Returned to the United States for weapons**: $12.2M[^18]; **diversion provable at trial**: $3.6M; Walsh wrote that the actual amount was probably at least $1.1M more, and that the exact total could not be determined because Enterprise funds were commingled[^19]
- **Price structure**: North and Secord negotiated a $10,000-per-missile price with Ghorbanifar, whose bridge financing came from Khashoggi, against approximately $3,700 charged per TOW including handling;[^20] the markup (about $6,300 per missile before expenses on the 1,000 TOWs sold in February 1986, and, on the 500 TOWs sold in October 1986, the difference between the $7,200 per TOW the Enterprise charged the Iranians and the $3,469 per TOW, plus shipping and handling, the United States charged) generated the surplus[^21]
- **Account**: Lake Resources Inc.[^43] (Geneva; managed by Willard Zucker/CSF)[^22]
- **Transfers**: Enterprise disbursements for weapons, ammunition, and operational costs for the Enterprise's Contra air delivery network[^23]

## The Discovery

On November 22, 1986, Justice Department attorneys reviewing NSC documents discovered a surviving copy of North's April 1986 diversion memorandum, and Meese was told of it during lunch.[^24] Meese's inquiry continued through the weekend while North continued destroying documents.[^25] On November 25, Meese publicly disclosed the diversion; Poindexter resigned and North was reassigned to the U.S. Marines.[^26]

## Walsh's Legal Theory

Walsh treated the diversion as financially integrating the Iran and Contra operations: the money obtained from diverting Iran arms-sale profits was used to support the conspirators' Enterprise in support of the Contras.[^27] This integration was the basis for the most serious legal theory Walsh pursued: the 18 U.S.C. §371 conspiracy to defraud the United States — Count One in the North case — which had three "objects":[^28]

1. The secret Contra war in defiance of Boland[^29]
2. The self-dealing slush fund (<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Secord</span> and Hakim's personal enrichment from the price spread)[^30]
3. The corruption of the Iran initiative by diverting it toward the enrichment and goals of the Enterprise.[^31]

Count One was dismissed on CIPA grounds before trial.[^32] But Walsh's report calls the Count One conspiracy charge "the central and perhaps the most important criminal charge developed by Independent Counsel."[^33]

## Hakim's Broader Diversion

Beyond the Contra diversion, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Albert Hakim</span> extended the Second Channel proposal beyond North's earlier plan and presidential policy by adding commitments to intercede with Kuwait for the release of Da'Wa prisoners.[^34] Walsh reported that the formal nine-point plan had been worked out by North, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Secord</span>, and Hakim, so the commitments were not unknown to all U.S. participants; the Iranians later insisted on adhering to it, with George Cave's apparent agreement.[^35]

## Open Questions

- Did North's early-April 1986 memorandum indicate that Reagan had approved (or would approve) the diversion, or did it seek Poindexter's approval without reference to the President?
- Given Poindexter’s testimony that he approved the diversion on his own authority,[^39] and Walsh’s assessment of the evidence concerning Reagan’s knowledge,[^40] what additional records could test Poindexter’s account?
- What incentives might have shaped Poindexter’s claim of sole responsibility, in light of Walsh’s assessment of the evidence concerning presidential knowledge?[^41]
- Given Walsh’s account of the Enterprise’s transfers through Amalgamated Commercial Enterprises and commingling in Lake Resources,[^42] what further transaction-level records could distinguish the amounts and channels of diverted Iran proceeds?
