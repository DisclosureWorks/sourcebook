# MHCHAOS

**Assessed.** *This page states a conclusion. Our assessment is below.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) program, officially framed as worldwide foreign collection, that in practice collected information on domestic American dissidents, 1967–1974; held files on some 7,200 to 7,500 Americans by the official counts (Colby spoke of a list of '9,900 plus' names) and indexed roughly 300,000 names[^1]

## Overview

MHCHAOS (also rendered as <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Operation CHAOS</span>) was a CIA counterintelligence program that was set up in August 1967 inside the Counterintelligence Staff headed by <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">James Jesus Angleton</span>, was run by Richard Ober's Special Operations Group, moved to the Operations Staff in 1973, and ended in March 1974.[^2] It was ostensibly predicated on the theory that the Soviet Union or other foreign powers were directing or funding the domestic U.S. peace and dissident movement.[^3] In practice, it became a large repository of information on American dissidents, drawn mainly from Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) reports and overt sources and well beyond what its foreign-links mission required.[^4]

The program was terminated in March 1974, nine months before Seymour Hersh's December 1974 exposé.[^5]

## Scale and Scope

Per the Wilderotter memo (dated January 3, 1975, recording a December 31, 1974 meeting) and <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Colby</span>'s January 3, 1975 briefing of President <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Ford</span>:[^6]

- Approximately **9,900–10,000 American names** in the files[^7]
- **About two-thirds of the names** on the "9,900 plus" list came from FBI requests or CIA foreign-station reports (per Colby)[^8]
- **The other one-third** of the names came from FBI reports on Americans in the peace movement[^9]
- The **Huston Plan** and the **[Intelligence Evaluation Committee](atlas-38bc98b898c98ef9ad8ab5081f8a7a6444bc0d2c672ea01916635d9b14985ce3.md) (IEC)** "gave stimulus" to the program — i.e., White House pressure drove its expansion[^10]
- Colby said CIA had apparently placed **agents in the peace movement** with the purported purpose of establishing credentials to travel abroad, and that information on domestic peace groups was a "by-product"; the Rockefeller Commission later found that on three occasions agents of the Operation were specifically used to collect domestic intelligence[^11]
- Colby considered the program "**worthless**" from an intelligence standpoint — among other things, the Soviets apparently thought U.S. dissidents "were too unruly to be trusted with any sensitive operations"[^12]

## Colby's Assessment and the Official Findings

Director of Central Intelligence (DCI) Colby judged MHCHAOS worthless from an intelligence standpoint.[^12] The Rockefeller Commission found that some of its domestic activities unlawfully exceeded CIA's statutory authority,[^17] and that the Agency's repeated reports found no significant foreign connection with domestic disorder.[^10]

## White House Connection

MHCHAOS was driven by White House demands: CIA set it up in August 1967 in response to presidential pressure for evidence of foreign influence on domestic dissent, and continuing requests from two Presidents, including Nixon White House requirements passed through <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">John Dean</span> and the Intelligence Evaluation Committee, added resources to it.[^13] White House requirements were channeled to CIA through <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">John Dean</span> and the IEC.[^14] The Family Jewels document notes that "The White House has insisted that the existence of this Committee be kept secret."[^15] This links the Nixon White House, through Dean's requirements to the Justice-chaired IEC staff, to the CHAOS office, whose chief was CIA's representative on that staff; the Rockefeller Commission found one instance in which he directed an agent to gather domestic information for it.[^16]

## Legal Analysis

The program implicated the National Security Act of 1947's restriction on CIA law-enforcement and internal-security functions.[^17] The CIA Act of 1949 separately supplied confidential fiscal and administrative authorities.[^18] The program appears in the Family Jewels, the compilation assembled after Director Schlesinger's May 1973 directive asking senior officials to report activities "which might be construed by reasonable people to be outside the legislative charter of this Agency."[^19]

## Church Committee Findings

The Church Committee (1975–1976) examined MHCHAOS extensively.[^20] The committee staff drew on CIA records, Helms's testimony, a Karamessines memorandum and Ober's estimate.[^21] The program became one of the committee's central examples of CIA domestic overreach.[^22]

## Open Questions

- To what extent did MHCHAOS drive the restrictions on CIA domestic activities in Executive Order 11905 (1976) and Executive Order 12333 (1981)?

- Were any of the Americans named in MHCHAOS files members of Congress?
- What happened to the MHCHAOS files after March 1974: were they destroyed, transferred to the FBI, or retained at the CIA?
- Which CIA agents inside the peace movement were publicly identified, and did any continue domestic political activity after MHCHAOS?
- Was MHCHAOS the central Domestic Surveillance item in the Family Jewels compilation, and how should centrality be distinguished from the scale of collection?
- Which MHCHAOS activities were unlawful, and what intelligence objectives did the program achieve?
- Beyond the improper tasking for the IEC described by the Rockefeller Commission, what broader command authority ran from the Nixon White House through the IEC to CIA domestic intelligence collection?
- What specific weight did Colby's testimony carry in the Church Committee's CHAOS chapter?
