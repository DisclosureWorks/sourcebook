# Congressional Oversight Typology (Haas 2x2)

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** A two-dimensional typology of congressional laws regulating CIA covert action, introduced by <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Melinda Haas</span> in "Origins of Oversight" (2023).[^1] The two dimensions are **authority** (enabling vs. prohibitive) and **doctrine** (procedural vs. substantive), yielding four cells.[^2]

## Overview

The typology labels laws regulating CIA covert operations on two dimensions:[^2]

| Dimension | Pole 1 | Pole 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Authority** | **Enabling** — expands or grants CIA freedom of action | **Prohibitive** — restricts CIA freedom of action[^3] |
| **Doctrine** | **Procedural** — regulates the decisionmaking process for covert action | **Substantive** — regulates a specific means or location of covert action[^4] |

The 2x2 cells are populated as follows for the 1947–2008 period (29 laws total per Haas's catalog):[^5]

|  | Prohibitive | Enabling |
|---|---|---|
| **Procedural** | 1953 DDCI Amendment, [Hughes-Ryan Amendment](atlas-273f511a70ee65148b5de2dc0bfc90677a8dfef583ffe4ee71e10bacfc7b20c9.md) (1974), SSCI creation (1976), HPSCI creation (1977), [IOA-1980](atlas-57fbb56c98455e69a6a52b3b6c5a4fff423bc09eb5ac43701cbee952f9162362.md), CIA IG Act (1989), [IAA-1991](atlas-01ddbdf46d2d12ac962e38e57c964bec9fb88d63ec48c12d04fd0604805944e3.md), IAA-FY-1997, Homeland Security Act (2002)[^6] | National Security Act (1947), CIA Act (1949), CIA Retirement Act (1964), Classified Information Procedures Act (1980), Intelligence Identities Protection Act (1982), CIA Information Act (1984), IRTPA-2004[^7] |
| **Substantive** | Tunney Amendment (1976), Clark Amendment (1976), [Boland Amendments 1–7](atlas-e19565a32ce0b3775b2ab13bc91c29a1c384c64c47dd81ee039091863d7a072c.md) (1982–85 per Haas's tables; her text counts five, 1982–86), Nunn-Mitchell Amendment (1994), NDAA-FY-2004[^8] | FISA (1978), Duncan Hunter NDAA-FY-2009[^9] |

## Analytical significance

Mathew McCubbins and Thomas Schwartz introduced the "police patrols versus fire alarms" framework in 1984;[^10] Loch Johnson separately described intelligence oversight as driven by scandal and shock.[^11]

**Two empirical claims supported by the typology:**

1. **Substantive laws tend to follow scandals.**[^12] Congress is more likely to choose substantive laws when faced with recent intelligence scandals;[^13] the Boland Amendments are Haas's example (a response to CIA support for the Nicaraguan Contras, including the mining of Nicaraguan harbors).[^14]
2. **Bipartisan support correlates negatively with prohibition.**[^15] When bipartisan support is strong, Congress tends to enact enabling laws; Haas documents this with IRTPA 2004 (House 183 Democrats and 152 Republicans in favor; Senate 44 Democrats and 51 Republicans).[^16] Haas argues that prohibitive laws can more easily come from the opposition party acting to weaken the CIA's ties to the executive branch, and that Congress is less likely to pass prohibitive laws as bipartisan support increases.[^17]

**Structural finding from the typology:** In Haas's 1947–2008 sample, none of the substantive-prohibitive laws amended the 1947 National Security Act;[^18] Congress instead used appropriations or separate authorization vehicles for substantive intervention.[^19] This is a descriptive finding about that sample, not proof that the 1947 Act is substantively unamendable.

## Open Questions

- Does the Haas typology generalize beyond Covert Action regulation to intelligence collection, analysis and counterintelligence, and how would its scope affect an account of congressional oversight?
- Given Haas's classification of laws on two dimensions,[^22] how should the typology account for a law that moves between cells through amendment?
- Does the novel typology Haas describes[^23] distinguish what Congress regulates from how it regulates in a way the McCubbins-Schwartz and Johnson accounts do not capture?
- Given Haas's critique of traditional oversight and her question about legislative form,[^24] how well do the McCubbins-Schwartz and Johnson accounts explain which form Congress chooses for a particular oversight gap?
