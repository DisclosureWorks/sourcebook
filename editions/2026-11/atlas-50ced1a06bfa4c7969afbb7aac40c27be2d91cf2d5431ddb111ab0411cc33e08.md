# Suspicious Activity Reports

Edition label: `2026-11`

**Definition:** A Suspicious Activity Report (**SAR**) is the regulatory filing that U.S. financial institutions are required by the [Bank Secrecy Act](atlas-27921b10cb3cdde5dc90a6846d7d74b734dcb0c276d434b5ccbfd41bab9a4381.md) (31 U.S.C. § 5318(g)) and Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) regulations to submit when they identify activity meeting the criteria for possible criminal conduct (including money laundering, structuring, and other suspected illicit financial activity).[^1] SARs are filed confidentially with FinCEN and are not disclosable to the subject of the report.[^2]

## Overview

SARs are the principal documentary artifact through which financial institutions discharge — or fail to discharge — their BSA detection-and-reporting obligation.[^3] The filing standard concerns a financial institution's knowledge or suspicion that a transaction meets specified regulatory criteria; the deadline is 30 calendar days after initial detection (extendable to 60 days when the subject is unknown).[^4]

## Epstein-related financial records

The cited Senate materials describe a **post-arrest retroactive-filing pattern** at JPMorgan: seven SARs covering about $4.3 million filed between 2002 and 2016, followed by two SARs filed on August 13 and September 26, 2019 (after Epstein's July 6, 2019 arrest and his August 10, 2019 death) covering about $1.3 billion in transactions dating back to 2003.[^5] A House letter describes the earlier period differently, saying JPMorgan filed no SAR at all before Epstein's death.[^17] The Senate Finance Committee staff memorandum quantifies JPMorgan's reporting before and after Epstein's arrest.[^6]

- [The Senate Finance Committee staff memorandum](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/memorandum_to_senator_wyden_on_jpmc-epstein_redactedpdf.pdf) reports **$4.3M flagged across 2002–2016 versus $1.3B retroactively across August–September 2019.**[^6] The memorandum calls the value of suspicious transactions JPMorgan reported after Epstein's death "nearly 300 times greater" than what it flagged while he was alive, about two and a half orders of magnitude.[^7]
- [Raskin, Jamie. Letter to Jamie Dimon, Chairman and Chief Executive Officer, JPMorgan Chase & Co. U.S. House Committee on the Judiciary, October 8, 2025.](https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-judiciary.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/2025-10-08.raskin-to-dimon-jpmorgan-re-epstein.pdf) — House Judiciary Committee Ranking Member letter framing JPMorgan's conduct as a SAR-filing failure: it says no SAR was filed during the relationship and that the first SARs (4,700 transactions, $1.1 billion) came only after Epstein's death, relying on N.Y. Times reporting.[^8]
- [Wyden, Ron. Letter to Pam Bondi, Attorney General; Scott Bessent, Secretary of the Treasury; and Kashyap Patel, Director, Federal Bureau of Investigation. U.S. Senate Committee on Finance, June 16, 2025.](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/wyden_doj_treasury_epstein_letter.pdf) — seeks FinCEN-compiled records on payments to Epstein and on banks' failure to report them in a timely fashion, which Treasury acknowledged holding but declined to produce; the letter does not use the term "SAR".[^9]
- [Government of the United States Virgin Islands. *Second Amended Complaint*. *Government of the United States Virgin Islands v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.*, No. 1:22-cv-10904-JSR (S.D.N.Y. filed April 12, 2023), ECF No. 119.](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.591653/gov.uscourts.nysd.591653.119.0.pdf) — civil action treating SAR-filing failure as predicate liability.[^10]

## SAR confidentiality vs. congressional access

SARs are statutorily confidential, with limited disclosure permitted to law enforcement and regulatory authorities.[^18][^11] The cited [Wyden Department of Justice (DOJ)/Treasury letter](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/wyden_doj_treasury_epstein_letter.pdf) describes a dispute over congressional access to FinCEN records.[^12]
