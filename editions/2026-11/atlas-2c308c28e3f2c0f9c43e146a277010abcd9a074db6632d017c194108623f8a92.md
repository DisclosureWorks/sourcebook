# National Security State

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The institutional architecture established by the National Security Act of 1947 and its companion statutes[^1] — the National Security Council[^2], the Central Intelligence Agency[^3], the unified Department of Defense[^4], and the supporting executive-branch infrastructure for intelligence, defense, and foreign-policy coordination[^5].

## Overview

The term is the label for the postwar U.S. institutional regime created by the National Security Act of 1947[^6] and elaborated through subsequent statutes (CIA Act 1949)[^7], executive orders[^8], and NSC directives.[^9] It is contrasted with the pre-1947 architecture, in which intelligence and defense functions were dispersed across military service intelligence offices (ONI, MID) and wartime organizations such as the OSS, with the Central Intelligence Group (1946-47) as the CIA's immediate precursor.[^10]

Scholarship on the national security state includes Michael Hogan's *A Cross of Iron* (1998) (Hogan, Michael J. *A Cross of Iron: Harry S. Truman and the Origins of the National Security State, 1945–1954*. Cambridge University Press, 1998.).[^11] Glennon's *National Security and Double Government* (2014) ([Glennon, Michael J. “National Security and Double Government.” *Harvard National Security Journal* 5 (2014): 1–114.](https://harvardnsj.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2014/01/Glennon-Final.pdf)) advances the "Trumanite-network" thesis: a permanent bureaucratic network operates with effective independence from the Madisonian institutions (the President, Congress, and the courts).[^12]

## Open Questions

- What is the empirical scale of the post-1947 national security state, measured by personnel, budget and operational footprint, across the 1947–2024 timeline? How would a documented growth curve bear on Glennon's Trumanite-network thesis, and how do the post-9/11 data in Priest and Arkin's *Top Secret America* compare with Cold War evidence?
- What further bureaucratic, doctrinal and operational practices did the National Security Council, Central Intelligence Agency and Department of Defense develop across the postwar era, beyond NSC 10/2's Covert Action procedure and the statutory architecture?
- Do disputes over bureaucratic incentives, permanent-emergency claims and compartmentation-driven oversight failures, and executive overreach, recur across administrations, or reflect the continuous intensification the Church Committee describes as "For three decades, these problems have grown more intense"?[^15]
- Does Glennon's Trumanite-network thesis extend Hogan's account of the national security state?
- Is "national security state" a standard scholarly label for the postwar U.S. institutional regime, given that Glennon treats it as part of a "popular conception" he calls "off the mark"?[^16] How widely, and since when, have historians used the term for that regime?
- Do Daniel Yergin's *Shattered Peace*, Douglas Stuart's *Creating the National Security State* and Andrew Bacevich's *Washington Rules* belong among the foundational scholarship on the national security state, and in which editions and years were they published?
