# Bank Secrecy Act

**Definition:** The Currency and Foreign Transactions Reporting Act of 1970 (codified at 31 U.S.C. § 5311 *et seq.*), commonly the **Bank Secrecy Act (BSA)** — the principal U.S. federal statute requiring financial institutions to maintain records of, and file reports on, transactions that may indicate money laundering, tax evasion, or other criminal activity.[^1]

## Overview

The BSA establishes the federal anti-money-laundering recordkeeping and reporting framework administered by the U.S. Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN).[^2] Two mandatory reporting mechanisms are:

- **Currency Transaction Reports (CTRs)** — required for any cash transaction over $10,000 in a single business day.[^3]
- **Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs)** — required when a financial institution identifies activity meeting regulatory criteria for possible criminal conduct.[^4] See [Suspicious Activity Reports](atlas-50ced1a06bfa4c7969afbb7aac40c27be2d91cf2d5431ddb111ab0411cc33e08.md).

The SAR deadline is generally 30 calendar days after initial detection of facts that may constitute a basis for filing, and may extend to 60 days only if no suspect is identified.[^5]

## Application to Epstein-related financial records

The cited congressional records describe a Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) asymmetry at JPMorgan, though they differ on the numbers: the Senate Finance Committee staff memorandum to Senator Wyden says JPMorgan filed 7 SARs flagging $4.3 million between 2002 and 2016, then two SARs in August and September 2019 flagging more than 5,000 wire transfers totaling about $1.3 billion, while Representative Raskin's letter says JPMorgan filed no SAR during the relationship and, after Epstein's death, filed SARs covering 4,700 transactions totaling $1.1 billion.[^6] The Bank of America complaint alleges SAR-filing failures.[^7]
