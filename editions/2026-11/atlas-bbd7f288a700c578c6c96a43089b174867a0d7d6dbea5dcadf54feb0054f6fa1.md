# Congressional Immunity Grants and Prosecution

**Assessed.** *This page states a conclusion. Our assessment is below.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The tension between Congress's use-immunity grants to compel testimony and subsequent criminal prosecution of the same witnesses, which Walsh's final report found illustrated "in an especially stark fashion" by the <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span> affair,[^20] where the D.C. Circuit reversed the convictions of <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Oliver North</span> and <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">John Poindexter</span> because the trial courts' safeguards did not ensure that witnesses' testimony was unaffected by the immunized congressional testimony[^1]

## Overview

The Fifth Amendment's privilege against self-incrimination can be overcome by a grant of use immunity: at Congress's request, a court orders the witness to testify and bars use of the compelled testimony against the witness in a criminal prosecution, so the privilege no longer applies.[^2] The limitation is that the immunized testimony — and any evidence derived from it — cannot be used against the witness in a criminal prosecution.[^3]

In <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span>, the House and Senate investigating committees each authorized use immunity for Oliver North and John Poindexter; Poindexter gave a closed deposition under an immunity order in May 1987, and the district court issued North's immunity order on June 15, 1987.[^4] <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Albert Hakim</span> was also granted immunity.[^5] These decisions were made during the Select Committees' joint investigation, after the Committees weighed their need for testimony against the Independent Counsel's objections.[^6]

## The Kastigar Standard and the D.C. Circuit Reversals

The governing Supreme Court precedent is *Kastigar v. United States* (1972), which held that the government bears the burden of proving that each piece of evidence used at trial was derived from a source independent of the immunized testimony — not just that the specific immunized testimony was not introduced.[^7]

**North reversal (July 1990)**: The D.C. Circuit reversed North's three convictions.[^8] The court held that testimony from witnesses whose memories had been refreshed by exposure to North's nationally televised immunized testimony was an improper use of it, and that the trial court had not held a sufficient hearing to establish, witness by witness, that each witness's testimony was untainted.[^9] The court remanded for detailed "taint" proceedings; after Robert McFarlane testified on remand that his trial testimony had been "colored" by North's immunized testimony, Independent Counsel consented to dismissal of the remaining counts on September 16, 1991.[^10]

**Poindexter reversal (November 1991)**: The D.C. Circuit reversed Poindexter's conviction on similar grounds.[^11] He was not retried: after the Supreme Court declined review in December 1992, the indictment was dismissed on Independent Counsel's motion in 1993.[^28] Walsh’s account at page 135 describes the reversal as resting on a trial “impermissibly tainted” by Poindexter’s immunized testimony.[^12] At page 564, Walsh says the appellate panels “did not find” that the prosecution was tainted, but held that the trial courts’ safeguards did not ensure that witnesses’ testimony was unaffected.[^27]

Then-Chief Judge Patricia Wald dissented in *North* (and again when the panel denied rehearing), warning that the majority's procedure "makes a subsequent trial of any congressionally immunized witness virtually impossible"; in *Poindexter*, Chief Judge Abner Mikva dissented in part.[^13] <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Walsh</span>'s Final Report quoted Wald's analysis approvingly.[^14]

## Walsh's Structural Critique

Walsh's Final Report contained a detailed structural critique of the immunity grant decision: Congress gave immunity to North and Poindexter "who incriminated only themselves and who largely exculpated those responsible for the initiation, supervision and support of their activities."[^15] The immunity grants allowed North and Poindexter to tell a story that pointed blame downward (at themselves) and not upward — at Reagan, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Bush</span>, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Weinberger</span>, and <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Shultz</span>.[^16] Walsh treated the damage as foreseeable: his office warned from the outset that immunity grants "would make prosecution of those persons problematic," and he concluded that Congress had "accepted the tendered concept of a runaway conspiracy of subordinate officers."[^17]

Worse: Walsh documented that Reagan himself "first asked that North and Poindexter be given immunity so that they could exculpate him from responsibility for the diversion."[^18] In Walsh's account, the Select Committees did what Reagan had asked a few months earlier, "granting immunity without any proffer to ensure honest testimony," to witnesses who "largely exculpated those responsible for the initiation, supervision and support of their activities."[^19]

## Implications for Oversight vs. Prosecution

In <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span>, Congress's grants of use immunity for nationally televised testimony ended the prosecutions of the two immunized principals, North and Poindexter, whose convictions were set aside and not retried; Walsh called the immunity decision "fatal to the prosecutions of North and Poindexter."[^20] In Walsh's account, Congress needs testimony to conduct oversight, while prosecutors need to preserve prosecution options.[^21] When Congress compels such testimony first, it can foreclose prosecution of those witnesses in highly publicized cases; Walsh warned that future immunity grants, "at least in such highly publicized cases, will likely rule out criminal prosecution."[^22]

Walsh's recommendation: Congress should compel public testimony from an official suspected of criminal misconduct "only in the most extraordinary circumstances," after asking whether a less culpable witness could supply the evidence and whether the need is compelling.[^23] Walsh nonetheless accepted that when oversight and prosecution conflict "it is Congress that must prevail," and he "has never contended that Congress should refrain from granting use immunity to compel testimony in such important matters as Iran/contra."[^24]
