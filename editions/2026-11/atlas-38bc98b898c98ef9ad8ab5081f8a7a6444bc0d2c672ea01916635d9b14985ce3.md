# Intelligence Evaluation Committee

**Definition:** White House-sponsored interagency intelligence coordination body (IEC), supported by the Intelligence Evaluation Staff (IES);[^1] documented in the Family Jewels as a channel for domestic intelligence studies;[^2] kept secret at White House insistence[^3]

## Overview

The Intelligence Evaluation Committee (IEC), supported by the Intelligence Evaluation Staff (IES), was an interagency body established under Nixon-era White House authority.[^4]

The Family Jewels includes a memorandum on possible embarrassment to the Agency and separately records the White House's secrecy instruction.[^5]

## White House Connection

The Family Jewels documentation is explicit: "The White House has insisted that the existence of this Committee be kept secret."[^6]

**<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">John Dean</span>'s role:** Per the Family Jewels, the IES received intelligence requirements from John Dean of the White House and delivered reports to him.[^7] Dean's documented role placed him in the channel for intelligence requirements and reports; the Church Committee nevertheless described the IEC as a research group with no operational dimension.[^8] This is the same John Dean who became a central Watergate figure and testified against Nixon in the Senate Watergate Committee.[^9]

## Domestic Intelligence Requirements

According to a January 3, 1975 Justice Department memorandum of a meeting with CIA Director William Colby, Colby said the "Huston Plan" and the establishment of the IEC "gave stimulus" to the CIA's domestic dissident program.[^10] On this page's reading, the record supports describing the IEC as one channel through which White House intelligence requirements reached the CIA; it does not establish what the CIA otherwise would have done or attribute the full 9,900-name file to IEC requirements.[^20]

## The Huston Plan Connection

The IEC operated in the context of the Huston Plan (1970) — Tom Charles Huston's proposal for coordinated domestic intelligence operations,[^11] approved by Nixon[^12] but then withdrawn under J. Edgar Hoover's objection.[^13] The IEC represented the institutional version of the coordination the Huston Plan had contemplated.[^14] Even after the formal Huston Plan was withdrawn, the IEC carried forward similar interagency coordination functions.[^15]
