**Definition:** The Trafficking Victims Protection Act of 2000 (Pub. L. No. 106-386)[^13], as reauthorized and amended (including by the William Wilberforce Trafficking Victims Protection Reauthorization Act of 2008) — a U.S. federal statutory framework for criminal prosecution and civil liability for sex trafficking.[^14] The criminal and civil provisions discussed here include 18 U.S.C. §§ 1591 and 1595; the Trafficking Victims Protection Act (TVPA) also includes provisions codified in Title 22; **18 U.S.C. § 1591** is the criminal Sex Trafficking offense[^1], and **18 U.S.C. § 1595** is the civil cause of action[^2].

## Overview

The TVPA's § 1591 establishes the federal Sex Trafficking offense;[^3] § 1595 creates a civil right of action for victims (and, importantly, for third-party "beneficiary" liability against persons or entities who knowingly benefited from participation in a venture they knew or should have known engaged in trafficking).[^4]

In the Epstein-network civil-liability record, Trafficking Victims Protection Act (TVPA) claims are central to the major financial-institution cases: in *Government of the U.S. Virgin Islands v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.*, the Virgin Islands sought relief under § 1595(d) for alleged violations of § 1591(a)(2)[^5], while *Jane Doe v. Bank of America, N.A.*, invokes § 1595(a), including its should-have-known beneficiary liability[^6]. Judge Rakoff's May 2023 motion-to-dismiss opinion in the JPMorgan and Deutsche Bank cases analyzes the plaintiffs' claims that the banks knowingly benefited from participating in Epstein's Sex Trafficking venture under § 1591(a)(2) (participation, knowledge or reckless disregard that force, fraud or coercion would be used, and a benefit)[^7], and addresses the Virgin Islands' standing under § 1595(d).[^8]
