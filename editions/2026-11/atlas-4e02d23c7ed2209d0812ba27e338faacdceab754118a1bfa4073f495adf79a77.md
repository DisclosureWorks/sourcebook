# CIPA — Classified Information Procedures Act

**Assessed.** *This page states a conclusion. Our assessment is below.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The Classified Information Procedures Act (1980) governs how classified information is handled in criminal trials;[^1] Section 6(e) gives the Attorney General unrestricted authority to refuse to declassify information essential to a prosecution —[^2] authority that <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Walsh</span> said becomes questionable when an independent counsel has jurisdiction;[^3] Attorney General Thornburgh used it to end the <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Fernandez</span> prosecution entirely[^4]

## Overview

The Classified Information Procedures Act (CIPA, 18 U.S.C. app. 3, enacted 1980) was designed to prevent "graymail" — the tactic by which defendants in national security cases threaten to disclose classified information at trial in order to force the government to drop charges.[^5] CIPA established procedures for in camera review of classified material, substitutions and summaries, and — critically — Attorney General authority to prevent classified information from being disclosed in court.[^6]

Section 6(e) provides that if the court orders disclosure of classified information over the government's objection, the AG may file a declaration that the information cannot be disclosed in the national interest.[^7] The consequence is that the court must then dismiss the charges unless it can fashion a remedy that doesn't require the classified disclosure.[^8]

## Walsh's Structural Finding

The CIPA §6(e) authority presents a specific problem in independent counsel cases.[^9] The AG is a member of the President's Cabinet.[^10] An independent counsel, by contrast, is specifically designed to investigate presidential administrations independently of the AG.[^11] But CIPA §6(e) gives the AG discretion that Walsh said lets him block almost any potentially embarrassing prosecution requiring the declassification of information.[^12]

Walsh concluded that the Attorney General's unrestricted CIPA §6(e) authority becomes questionable when an independent counsel has jurisdiction.[^13] In Walsh's account, the problem is structural: the official who controls declassification may belong to the administration the independent counsel is investigating.[^14]

## The Fernandez Case

The Fernandez prosecution was, in Walsh's account, the first case an Attorney General ended by filing a CIPA §6(e) affidavit:[^15]

**The evidence**: Telephone records documented hundreds of calls among Fernandez, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Oliver North</span>, and Rafael Quintero;[^16] separately identified KL-43 messages showed encrypted operational communications.[^17] Together, this evidence supported Fernandez's coordination role in the Costa Rica southern front.[^18]

**The defense claim**: Fernandez argued it was critical to show what CIA headquarters and high-ranking CIA officials understood about the North–Secord contra-resupply operation, because their knowledge made it less likely that he had lied intentionally —[^19] a defense that also relied on classified program details and the identities of CIA stations and facilities.[^20]

**The §6(e) declaration**: On November 22, 1989, Attorney General Thornburgh filed a CIPA §6(e) affidavit barring disclosure at trial of information about three classified CIA programs and the identity of CIA stations and facilities in Central America; after the Fourth Circuit affirmed, the Justice Department told the court on October 12, 1990 that he would not withdraw it.[^21] The refusal covered the information the court had held essential to Fernandez's defense; Walsh accepted that the three CIA programs had to stay secret, but argued that the location of two well-known CIA stations, which the prosecution also needed to try the remaining charges, was already public.[^22]

**The dismissal**: Judge Claude Hilton (E.D. Virginia) dismissed all four counts.[^23]

Walsh's analysis uses the Fernandez prosecution as an example of the problem CIPA poses for independent-counsel cases.[^24]

## North's Counts One and Two

Before the Fernandez dismissal, the Reagan Administration's refusal to declassify information had already forced the dismissal of North's Count One —[^25] perhaps the most important charge:[^26] the 18 U.S.C. §371 conspiracy to defraud the United States (three objects: secret Contra war, self-dealing slush fund, corruption of Iran initiative).[^27] Count One, with Count Two (theft), was dismissed on January 13, 1989, on Independent Counsel's own motion, after the Administration refused to declassify information material to North's defense and the Attorney General filed an affidavit confirming the need to withhold it.[^28]

## Implications

Under CIPA §6(e), an Attorney General's refusal to permit disclosure of classified information a court has held essential to the defense can end a prosecution, as it did in the Fernandez case and for the central conspiracy and theft counts against North.[^29] Walsh located the problem in prosecutions "steeped in highly classified information."[^2]

Walsh criticized the absence of meaningful consultation with the independent counsel[^30] and concluded that "Congress could not have intended" CIPA to be used by the Attorney General "to control prosecutions of independent counsel."[^31]

## Open Questions

- Has subsequent legislation addressed the CIPA §6(e) problem Walsh identified?
- In post-<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span> independent counsel/special counsel cases (<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Starr</span>, Fitzgerald, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Mueller</span>, Smith), has CIPA §6(e) been used to block or shape prosecutions in ways similar to Thornburgh's actions?
- Is the Attorney General appointed by, and responsible to, the President?
- Is it a general rule that a case cannot be tried when both the prosecution and the defense need classified material the Attorney General refuses to release?
- Were the KL-43 messages in the Fernandez case identified by, or otherwise attributable to, the National Security Agency?
