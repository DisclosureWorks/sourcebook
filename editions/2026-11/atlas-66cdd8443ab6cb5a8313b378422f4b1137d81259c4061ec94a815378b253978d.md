# Richard Welch Assassination (1975) and the Ford White House Response

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** December 23, 1975 assassination of Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) Athens station chief <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Richard Welch</span> (the Revolutionary Organization of November 17 later claimed responsibility for the killing),[^1] and what Olmsted describes as the administration's propaganda campaign (from the body's arrival on or about December 30, 1975, through the Arlington funeral reported January 7, 1976).[^2]

## Overview

Welch had previously served as CIA station chief in Lima, Peru, where he had been identified by the Peruvian press.[^3] *Counterspy* magazine (Winter 1975) repeated the Lima identification[^4] (Marks's "How to Spot a Spook" (1974) had shown how to identify CIA officers from government publications).[^5] By that time Welch had been transferred to Athens, where he occupied the same residence used by his CIA predecessors.[^6] An Athens newspaper identified him as the station chief and printed his name, address, and phone number.[^7] He was assassinated December 23, 1975 outside his home.[^8]

**The Ford White House (WH) propaganda campaign** (per Olmsted (1996)):[^2]

- The plane carrying Welch's body was held to circle Andrews Air Force Base for fifteen minutes so it could land live on the morning news (on or about December 30, 1975).[^9]
- Olmsted reports that Ford waived burial restrictions to allow Welch interment at Arlington National Cemetery (over Veterans of Foreign Wars protests; Arlington is usually reserved for those who served in the armed forces).[^10]
- The Arlington funeral (reported January 7, 1976) used the horse-drawn caisson that had carried President Kennedy's body, in front of the assembled "American national security establishment."[^11]
- *The Washington Post* ran thirteen Welch stories in the week after his death; a *Post* editorial called the murder "the entirely predictable result of the disclosure tactics chosen by certain American critics of the agency".[^12]
- President and his spokesmen carefully avoided directly blaming the congressional investigators.[^13]

## Historical significance

The Welch episode is a central example in Olmsted's argument that the Ford administration mobilized against the intelligence investigations.[^14] Four retrospective first-person accounts from participants[^15][^16][^17][^18]:

- <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Rogovin</span> (CIA Special Counsel, March 1992 interview): WH "waved it around like a bloody shirt"; the not-quite-said implication was "Senator Church, you've got blood on your hands"; "loudly unsaid."[^15]
- <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Aaron Donner</span> (Pike Committee general counsel, September 1992): WH handled the death "magnificently, in a malevolent sort of way"; "I don't think a president could be buried with more pomp and circumstance".[^16]
- <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">F.A.O. Schwarz, Jr.</span> (Church Committee chief counsel, August 1992): WH "danced with joy on his grave. . . . Not that they were glad he was dead. They just exploited it in an utterly inappropriate way".[^17]
- <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Otis Pike</span> (June 1992): WH played the press "like an organ".[^18]

Olmsted relies on these four accounts — from the House committee chair and senior committee and CIA counsel — in arguing that the Welch affair demonstrated the Ford White House's "command over the media," and that the president's ability to control secret information gave him a significant advantage over his congressional opponents.[^19]

Olmsted treats the Welch operation as the **turning point** of the 1975–76 investigations:[^20] after Welch, congressional investigators were on the defensive;[^21] the eventual House vote 246–124 to suppress the Pike Report was made significantly easier by congressmen's fear of being associated with "the disclosure of any secrets."[^22]

## Open Questions

- Was the Athens-newspaper identification of Welch independent of Counterspy's Lima identification, or a republication?
- Did the Intelligence Coordinating Group coordinate the Ford White House's publicity around the return of Welch's body and his funeral?
- Did the Welch funeral publicity constitute a deliberate structural attack on the Pike and Church investigations?
- To what extent do Rogovin's, Donner's, Schwarz's and Pike's retrospective accounts agree in characterizing the Welch funeral publicity?
