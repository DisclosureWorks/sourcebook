# Covert Action

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** Government activity designed to influence political, economic, or military conditions abroad in ways where the sponsoring government's role is not intended to be apparent or acknowledged publicly.[^1] Distinct from intelligence collection and from overt foreign policy.[^2]

## Statutory Definition

The current statutory definition (50 U.S.C. § 3093), first enacted in the Intelligence Authorization Act FY1991 (P.L. 102-88), reads:[^3]

> "[A]n activity or activities of the United States Government to influence political, economic, or military conditions abroad, where it is intended that the role of the United States Government will not be apparent or acknowledged publicly."[^27][^4]

Explicitly excluded from the definition: traditional diplomatic and military activities, traditional law enforcement activities, and traditional counterintelligence activities.[^5] The statutory definition was enacted in 1991 as part of the legislation that followed <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Iran-Contra</span>; the conference report described it as a clarification, "not to relax or go beyond previous understandings."[^6]

## Typology

The CRS report identifies four principal categories—propaganda, political action, economic activities, and paramilitary operations—while the discussion below separately groups several methods:[^7]

1. **Propaganda** — including "black" propaganda (false attribution), "gray" propaganda (unattributed), and "white" propaganda (openly attributed influence)[^28]
2. **Political action** — funding political parties, elections, civil society organizations, or media outlets[^8]
3. **Paramilitary operations** — training, arming, and directing foreign forces; proxy warfare[^9]


## Authorization Framework

**Presidential Finding:** Every covert action requires a written "finding" signed by the President determining that the action is necessary to support identifiable foreign policy objectives and is important to the national security of the United States.[^10] Findings cannot be retroactive.[^11] This requirement was established by the Intelligence Authorization Act FY1991 in response to the Reagan administration's retroactive Iran-Contra finding.[^12]

**Gang of Eight notification:** For extraordinary cases, the President may limit notification to eight congressional leaders rather than the full intelligence committees.[^13] Restricted notification can prevent members from consulting most staff or colleagues, which can impede oversight, but Congress retains legislative and appropriations powers.[^14]

**Privatization to avoid accountability:** Some observers suggest that covert efforts are sometimes "privatized", for example to a private consulting firm, precisely to avoid notification to congressional committees.[^15]

**Legislative pre-authorization (Congress directing Covert Action funding over administration objection):** Two cases in the 1990s — the FY1996 Intelligence Authorization Act's classified-Schedule Iran provision (~$18M; P.L. 104-93, January 6, 1996, [*Intelligence Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 1996*. Pub. L. No. 104-93, 109 Stat. 961 (January 6, 1996). H.R. 1655, 104th Cong.](https://www.congress.gov/104/plaws/publ93/PLAW-104publ93.pdf)) and the Iraq Liberation Act of 1998 (P.L. 105-338, [U.S. Congress. *Iraq Liberation Act of 1998*. Pub. L. No. 105-338, 112 Stat. 3178 (October 31, 1998). H.R. 4655, 105th Cong.](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-105publ338/pdf/PLAW-105publ338.pdf)) — saw Congress initiate operational priorities over administration objection.[^16] Neither statute bypassed the FY1991 IAA Findings architecture.[^17] The FY1996 IAA's §302 provides that the Act's authorization of appropriations is not itself authority for any intelligence activity not otherwise authorized by law; the Iran $18M was placed in the classified portion of the authorization as a Congress-initiated funding increment.[^18] The Iraq Liberation Act is structurally an *overt foreign-assistance authorization* (FAA §634A reporting via §4(d) and §5(d), not Covert Action procedures); §8 disclaims authorizing the use of U.S. Armed Forces, except for the §4(a)(2) military drawdown and training assistance.[^19] The "covert action" framing applies to the earlier CIA program with the INC under a separate 1991 executive Finding (ended in 1996-97), not to the ILA-funded activities, which the State Department administered.[^20] Congress signaled operational priority through statutory or budgetary direction (the Iran $18M provision leaked to the press before the President signed the bill; after the ILA passed, SSCI Chairman Richard Shelby disputed how its funds were allocated and threatened to block further spending).[^21] Cormac and co-authors, citing a former senior CIA counter-terrorism officer, report that members of Congress responsible for oversight 'want' to see a covert action they have supported succeed.[^22]
