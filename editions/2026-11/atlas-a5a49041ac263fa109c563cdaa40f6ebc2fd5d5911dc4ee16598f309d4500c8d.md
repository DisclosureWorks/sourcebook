# Fourth Amendment Doctrine

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

**Definition:** The body of constitutional law interpreting the Fourth Amendment's prohibition against "unreasonable searches and seizures" and its warrant clause.[^1]

## Overview

The Fourth Amendment text has remained unchanged since 1791,[^19] but its operative doctrine has shifted dramatically.[^2] *Olmstead* exemplified a property-based approach.[^3] *Katz* recognized protection based on reasonable expectations of privacy[^4] while the property-based test survived; *Jones* reaffirmed that the two tests are alternative paths to Fourth Amendment protection.[^17]

This page covers three legal rules: (1) the warrant requirement of *Katz*, subject to recognized exceptions;[^5] (2) the *Keith* domestic-security warrant requirement;[^6] and (3) FISA's statutory warrant regime for foreign-intelligence surveillance, which addresses, for foreign intelligence, the national-security question *Katz* footnote 23 reserved.[^15]

## National-security application

The Church Committee Book II (1976) documented systematic constitutional and statutory abuses in domestic intelligence programs, including Fourth Amendment infringements through surveillance and mail opening and First Amendment injuries from COINTELPRO.[^7] Later national-security litigation presents a distinct enforcement problem: state-secrets dismissals,[^8] standing barriers,[^9] and, after *Abbasi*, the Court's treatment of expanding the *Bivens* remedy as a disfavored judicial activity.[^20]

## Open Questions

- **Question:** Given that *Klayman v. Obama* (D.D.C. Dec. 2013) reached the Fourth Amendment question at an early stage; *ACLU v. Clapper* (2d Cir. 2015) decided the case on statutory grounds and expressly did not reach the constitutional issues,[^16] has any later merits ruling settled the constitutional question?
