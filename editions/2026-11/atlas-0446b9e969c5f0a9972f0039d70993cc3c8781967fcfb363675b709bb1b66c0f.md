# Epstein Files Transparency Act

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** Federal legislation (H.R. 4405; Pub. L. No. 119-38) requiring the Department of Justice (DOJ) to release covered unclassified Epstein-related records within 30 days, subject to five categories of permitted withholding or redaction in § 2(c).[^1]

## Overview

The Epstein Files Transparency Act passed Congress nearly unanimously during 2025, despite <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Trump</span>'s alleged efforts to defeat it.[^2] Trump signed the bill on November 19, 2025, setting a 30-day production deadline of December 19, 2025.[^3] DOJ missed that deadline.[^4] On January 30, 2026, DOJ said it had identified more than 6 million potentially responsive pages and released more than 3 million that day, bringing total releases to nearly 3.5 million after deduplication and responsiveness review.[^5]

The Act created separate legal obligations from the August 5, 2025 HOGR subpoena.[^6] <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Garcia</span> repeatedly emphasized that compliance with the EFTA did not satisfy the Committee's subpoena, which in his reading required production of the complete unredacted files including classified materials and information relevant to ongoing investigations.[^7]

## Implementation and disputes

**The "ongoing investigations" loophole:** Garcia alleged that DOJ based its withholdings and redactions largely on the Act's provision permitting withholding of information relevant to ongoing investigations, and that politically motivated investigations of political opponents are not a legitimate basis for withholding.[^8]

**DOJ's stated accounting:** DOJ's prior HOGR production was about 33,000 pages, described by Committee Democrats as largely already public.[^9]

**DOJ volume claims:** DOJ gave differing figures — 1M+ documents in a newly discovered tranche, then a reported 5.2M pages, then 2M+ documents in various phases of review.[^10]

**<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Bondi</span>'s contradictions pre-Act:** Before the Act was signed, Bondi said the client list was "sitting on my desk right now to review" (Feb 2025), then DOJ reversed and said its review "revealed no incriminating 'client list'" (July 2025 memo).[^11] She also claimed DOJ had "tens of thousands of videos of Epstein with children or child porn" — a claim Committee Democrats say FBI Director Patel contradicted.[^12] The July 2025 DOJ-FBI memo stated that the review found no evidence that could predicate an investigation of uncharged third parties.[^13]

**Congressional subpoena vs. EFTA:** The HOGR Aug 5, 2025 subpoena is an independent legal obligation.[^14] The EFTA permits narrowly tailored, temporary withholding for active investigations; the subpoena contains no such exception, though its schedule permits "any other redactions required by law".[^15][^16] Garcia argued that DOJ remained separately obligated to comply with the HOGR subpoena;[^47] this page identifies no court ruling that the subpoena superseded EFTA exceptions.

- Which active federal investigations or ongoing prosecutions, if any, underlie DOJ's Category C withholdings and redactions, and how do their predicates relate to the July 6 memorandum's finding on uncharged third parties? - Why did DOJ report using no national-security classification exemption under Sec. 2(c)(1)(E), and what does that choice establish about the records reviewed?[^45]
- Why were National Security Division and Criminal Division attorneys both assigned to first-level review, and how was work allocated among them?[^33]
- DOJ published its February 14, 2026 report in the Federal Register on August 27, 2026, and says the notice publishes the report pursuant to Section 2 of the Act.[^50] DOJ's January 30 letter said its obligations would be completed after "submitting the formal report to Congress" and "publishing the written justifications in the Federal Register".[^34] Does the report's category-level explanation provide the written justification for all redactions required by Sec. 2(c)(2), and what evidence establishes submission of those justifications to Congress?[^51]
- What does the Section 3 report's submission to the House and Senate Judiciary Committees, the statutory recipients, establish about the relationship between obligations under the Act and the parallel HOGR subpoena?[^46]
- Has DOJ produced the privilege log that its December 19 letter promised "in due course"?[^35]
- Did Garcia warn, when the Act passed, that Trump would exploit its "ongoing investigations" withholding provision, and did Trump direct DOJ to use that provision to open politically motivated investigations of political opponents?

**Congressional accountability:**

- Which members of Congress have visited DOJ to inspect unredacted materials?[^36]

- What is in the roughly 2.5 million potentially responsive pages that DOJ had identified but not released as of January 30, 2026, how much is duplicate, nonresponsive, privileged, foreign-language or technically incompatible material, and does DOJ's accounting hold up?[^38]

## DOJ's Section 3 Report (2026-02-14)

The Section 3 report provides the DOJ's official compliance accounting.[^19] DOJ published the February 14, 2026 letter as the appendix to an August 27, 2026 Federal Register notice, which it describes as publication pursuant to Section 2.[^50] The appendix keeps the February date and is not a newly dated August report.[^50] Its closing carries Bondi's and Blanche's typed names but does not reproduce the handwritten signature visible above Blanche's name in the archived February letter.[^52] Key findings from that document[^20]:

- **Nine release categories** confirmed, covering Epstein, Maxwell, flight logs, associated individuals, entities, immunity deals, internal DOJ charging communications, evidence-concealment records, and death documentation.[^20]
- **Three privileges invoked** for withheld material: deliberative-process, work-product, and attorney-client.[^21]
- **"active federal investigation" carve-out (Sec. 2(c)(1)(C))** listed among the grounds of DOJ's redaction process — the report quotes this category but does not identify any investigation or say how many redactions rest on it, so no contradiction with the July 2025 memo is established here.[^49]
- **PEP list** of roughly 300 government officials and politically exposed persons included, with DOJ caveating that omissions are "unintentional" and that names redacted for law-enforcement sensitive purposes are excluded.[^22]
- **Two Prior EFTA Letters** referenced: December 19, 2025 and January 29, 2026.[^23]
