# CIA Domestic Surveillance

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** Umbrella concept covering CIA activities directed against people in the United States, including mail opening and collection of information on Americans and domestic groups, documented in the Family Jewels and the Church Committee's findings.[^32]

## Overview

CIA domestic surveillance is not a single program but a cluster of related activities that the Family Jewels document assembled under the premise that they might be "outside the legislative charter" of the Agency.[^1] The National Security Act of 1947 restricted the Agency from exercising police, subpoena, or law-enforcement powers;[^2] the CIA Act of 1949 separately supplied fiscal and administrative authorities.[^3]

Despite this statutory restriction, the CIA conducted extensive domestic activities over approximately two decades (1953–1974) spanning mail opening, drug testing on unwitting persons, a break-in at domestic premises, and accumulated intelligence files on thousands of Americans and computer indexes holding hundreds of thousands of names (and, from mail opening, nearly one and a half million names).[^4]

## White House Intelligence Requirements

1. **Nixon White House** (via <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">John Dean</span> and Huston Plan)[^18] → 
2. **[Intelligence Evaluation Committee](atlas-38bc98b898c98ef9ad8ab5081f8a7a6444bc0d2c672ea01916635d9b14985ce3.md) (IEC)** (secret)[^19] → 
3. **CIA CI Staff** (<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Angleton</span>)[^20] → 
4. **[MHCHAOS](atlas-91b3b73bb74eb0bc1e5686c2a190dfae60edd66f7d6383ae94895d75d6734366.md)**[^21]

In the cited records, MHCHAOS and the IEC-related reporting operated in response to White House requirements: the Rockefeller Commission found that Operation CHAOS acted under presidential pressure, and the IEC staff took requirements from John Dean of the White House.[^22] The [MHCHAOS](atlas-91b3b73bb74eb0bc1e5686c2a190dfae60edd66f7d6383ae94895d75d6734366.md) page carries the graded assessment of this conclusion. The White House records also document the "blood will flow" warning and the <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Kissinger</span>/Helms concerns about containing disclosure (WH2/WH3).[^23]

## Oversight of These Activities

- **Congressional oversight was informal, exerted through subcommittees of the Appropriations Committees and the Armed Services Committees**[^24]
- **The cited record documents little contemporaneous judicial review of these activities**[^25]

The Church Committee found that 'plausible denial' was expanded to mask presidential decisions and that sensitive wishes could be conveyed indirectly rather than by written order.[^26]

## Post-1975 Oversight Architecture

The page cites the following post-1975 oversight instruments:

- **Executive Order 11905** (<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Ford</span>, 1976) — executive regulation of CIA domestic activities (§ 5, Restrictions on Intelligence Activities)[^27]
- **Executive Order 12333** (Reagan, 1981) — framework for United States intelligence activities, including restrictions on domestic activities[^28]
- **Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA, 1978)** — statutory framework for foreign-intelligence electronic surveillance warrants inside the United States[^29]
- **Intelligence Oversight Act of 1980** — congressional notification requirements[^30]
- **Senate Select Committee on Intelligence / House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence** — permanent oversight bodies created in 1976–1977[^31]
