# Hamas-for-PLO Doctrine

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

**Definition:** In his 1994 memoir, <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Victor Ostrovsky</span> argues that Mossad elements saw political advantage in Palestinian fundamentalists displacing the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO).[^1] This is his retrospective interpretation, not an authenticated institutional directive or a recorded 1986 statement by an intelligence officer.[^1]

## Source

[Ostrovsky, Victor. *The Other Side of Deception: A Rogue Agent Exposes the Mossad’s Secret Agenda*. First edition. New York: HarperCollins, 1994. ISBN 0-06-017635-0. Held electronic version.](https://archive.org/details/othersideofdecep00ostr), Chapter 22, PDF pp. 189–193.[^2] The Hamas passage is Ostrovsky’s narration, preceding the later June 29 conversation with Ephraim; the Jordan discussion is a separate episode in Chapter 18.[^24] The following is an unquoted paraphrase of the author’s interpretation[^3]:

Ostrovsky interprets support for fundamentalist groups as serving an alleged Mossad strategy of obstructing negotiations and displacing the PLO.[^3] The paragraph is the author’s retrospective analysis, not dialogue attributed to Ephraim.[^3]

U.S. Treasury testimony by David D. Aufhauser, under “HAMAS,” dates the organization’s formation to 1987.[^4] Ostrovsky’s 1994 memoir uses the name retrospectively in a narrative set in 1986;[^25] that usage does not establish a contemporary policy toward Hamas’s precursors.

## Operational mechanisms per Ostrovsky

[Ostrovsky, Victor. *The Other Side of Deception: A Rogue Agent Exposes the Mossad’s Secret Agenda*. First edition. New York: HarperCollins, 1994. ISBN 0-06-017635-0. Held electronic version.](https://archive.org/details/othersideofdecep00ostr) also describes alleged operations that Ostrovsky relates to this interpretation.[^5] The descriptions below remain memoir allegations, without independent operational records:[^6]

### Weapons pipeline to Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood

In [Ostrovsky, Victor. *The Other Side of Deception: A Rogue Agent Exposes the Mossad’s Secret Agenda*. First edition. New York: HarperCollins, 1994. ISBN 0-06-017635-0. Held electronic version.](https://archive.org/details/othersideofdecep00ostr), Chapter 22, PDF p. 192, Ostrovsky describes an alleged weapons route to the **Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood**.[^7] The following details are the memoir’s account, without independent primary corroboration here[^7]:

1. Ostrovsky says that much of the weaponry, which he describes as the Mujahideen’s and American-made, was supplied to the Muslim Brotherhood directly from Israel, carried by **Bedouin nomads** in the Sinai demilitarized zones.[^7]
2. Ostrovsky adds that captured Soviet-made PLO weapons **could** also have been supplied; he does not establish a specific delivery from those stocks or identify Mossad as the force that seized them.[^8]
4. **Alleged payment model:** Ostrovsky says that weapons were exchanged for targets to be attacked in Egypt; the passage identifies no particular completed assassination.[^9]

Ostrovsky alleges an arms-for-targets arrangement.[^10] The passage does not identify a contemporaneous contract, delivery record or particular assassination carried out under it.

### Jordan-destabilization companion doctrine

Ostrovsky’s alleged Jordan plan[^11] and his analysis of fundamentalist groups[^12] share a theme. Comparing these passages from the same memoir does not independently establish a formally adopted institutional doctrine.

### Peace-accord-sabotage context

Ostrovsky interprets support for fundamentalist groups as a means of obstructing peace negotiations and preserving a perceived external threat.[^13] This is the author’s analysis of motives, not proof that negotiations were impossible or that Mossad adopted such a policy.[^13]

Ostrovsky reports an allegation of sabotage of a Jordanian peace initiative in a narrative set before his May 1986 Jordan visit,[^14] and separately reports an allegation about the **1982** killings of Ray and Barsimantov.[^15] The former cannot be identified as the 1987 London Agreement on this evidence. The identity-unresolved alleged operative is omitted pending a primary record.

## Significance

On this page’s reading, the memoir’s argument raises a question about alleged support for fundamentalist groups.[^16] It does not establish that Mossad created Hamas, caused its emergence or adopted a formal doctrine governing Palestinian politics.[^16]

The memoir’s interpretation requires independent corroboration before conclusions can be drawn about institutional policy; the following comparison should be read with that limitation.

1. If corroborated, the allegation would suggest cultivation of rival political groups; this memoir alone does not demonstrate an adopted Mossad policy.[^17]
2. Comparisons with <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Operation Brush-Fire</span> concern narratives attributed to the same author.[^26] The cited Hamas passage cannot independently corroborate those narratives or establish a shared institutional policy.[^18]
