# Contempt of Congress

**Definition:** The statutory and constitutional mechanism by which Congress compels testimony and document production from recalcitrant witnesses, backed by potential criminal prosecution.[^1]

## Overview

Under 2 U.S.C. § 192, a witness summoned before Congress who willfully refuses to appear or answer is guilty of a misdemeanor punishable by a fine up to $100,000 and up to one year imprisonment (Class A misdemeanor under 18 U.S.C. §§ 3559(a)(6) and 3571(b)(5)).[^2] Under 2 U.S.C. § 194, the Speaker certifies a House contempt statement to the appropriate U.S. attorney,[^3] whose statutory duty is to present it to a grand jury.[^17]

The Supreme Court has affirmed that "the power of inquiry—with process to enforce it—is an essential and appropriate auxiliary to the legislative function" (*McGrain v. Daugherty*, 273 U.S. 135, 174 (1927)).[^4] A legislative body "cannot legislate wisely or effectively in the absence of information respecting the conditions which the legislation is intended to affect or change" (*McGrain*, 273 U.S. at 175; *Eastland v. United States Servicemen's Fund*, 421 U.S. 491, 504 (1975) (quoting *McGrain*)).[^5] The contempt mechanism requires criminal intent — a "deliberate, intentional refusal to answer" (*Quinn v. United States*, 349 U.S. 155, 165 (1955)).[^6]

## Application in the House Epstein investigation

The Clinton contempt report sets out the contempt mechanism at length in the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform (HOGR) <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Epstein</span> investigation.[^7] Key legal principles documented[^7]:

- A witness may not impose their own conditions on the manner of congressional inquiry (*Eisler v. United States*, 170 F.2d 273 (D.C. Cir. 1948); *United States v. Costello*, 198 F.2d 200 (2d Cir. 1952); *United States v. Hintz*, 193 F.Supp. 325 (N.D. Ill. 1961)).[^8]
- Ongoing Department of Justice (DOJ) criminal or civil investigations do not halt congressional oversight authority (*Sinclair v. United States*, 279 U.S. 263 (1929); *Hutcheson v. United States*, 369 U.S. 599 (1962)).[^9]
- In the Committee's view, Separation of Powers concerns and executive privilege may support question-by-question objections during testimony, not wholesale refusal to appear; Clinton's counsel had invoked *<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Trump</span> v. Mazars USA, LLP*, 591 U.S. 848 (2020), for the opposite position.[^10]
- The committee alone sets the terms of its oversight — which witnesses to interview, in what order, and in what format[^11] — and "a witness does not have the legal right to dictate the conditions under which he will or will not testify" (*Costello*).[^12]

**Other contempt proceedings:**
