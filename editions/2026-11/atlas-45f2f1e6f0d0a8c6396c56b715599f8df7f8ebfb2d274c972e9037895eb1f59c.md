# Foreign Intelligence Surveillance

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** Electronic surveillance conducted to acquire foreign-intelligence information, distinguished from criminal-investigation surveillance and (post-*Keith*) from domestic-security surveillance.[^1]

## Overview

The category is constitutionally distinct because *Katz v. United States* (1967) footnote 23 expressly reserved the question of whether warrantless surveillance for "national security" purposes might be permissible[^2] — a reservation that *Keith* (1972) closed for *domestic* security[^3] but explicitly left open for foreign-power surveillance.[^4] The 1972–1978 gap, during which only lower federal courts addressed warrantless foreign-intelligence wiretaps, was filled by the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978, which established the FISA Court and a statutory warrant regime for surveillance targeted at foreign powers and their agents.[^5]

Several courts of appeals have addressed warrantless foreign-intelligence surveillance on the merits;[^6] the Fourth Circuit held in *United States v. Truong Dinh Hung* (1980) that the executive need not always obtain a warrant for foreign-intelligence surveillance.[^7] The post-9/11 STELLARWIND program asserted inherent authority via OLC opinions (Yoo, beginning with a September 17, 2001 memorandum (expanded October 4, 2001) and a first formal opinion dated November 2, 2001, and subsequent);[^8] the Wyden-Udall correspondence and the PCLOB Section 215 report (2014) document a related critique, directed at the secret statutory interpretation of Section 215 that later carried the bulk telephone-records strand.[^9] The 2008 FISA Amendments Act and Section 702 created a statutory framework for surveillance targeting non-U.S. persons reasonably believed to be located abroad, partially codifying the post-9/11 architecture.[^10]

The executive characterized bulk metadata collection as "foreign intelligence" even when the metadata was overwhelmingly domestic.[^11]

## Analytical significance

Direct primary evidence appears in the *Katz* footnote-23 reservation, the *Keith* "express no opinion" on foreign-power surveillance, FISA 1978's foreign-intelligence-only scope, and the post-9/11 STELLARWIND / Section 215 architecture.[^12] In the Wyden-Udall correspondence, the senators objected publicly only in general terms to secret interpretations of Section 215 and did not describe the collection those interpretations authorized.[^13]

## Open Questions

- **Question:** In light of the PCLOB’s quotation of the 1978 House report and CRS’s 2007 account of the unresolved constitutional issue, has any later Supreme Court decision decided whether the President has inherent authority to conduct warrantless foreign-intelligence surveillance independent of FISA?[^14]
- **Question:** Has the executive branch ever articulated a doctrinal limit on what counts as "foreign intelligence surveillance" sufficient to fall outside *Keith* and within *Katz* fn-23?
