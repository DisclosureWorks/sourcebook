# SAR Failure

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** As described in the Senate Finance Committee reports and the Bank of America complaint, inadequate, late, or absent **[Suspicious Activity Report](atlas-50ced1a06bfa4c7969afbb7aac40c27be2d91cf2d5431ddb111ab0411cc33e08.md)** filings by U.S. financial institutions during their active relationship with <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Jeffrey Epstein</span> and his counterparties[^1] — which the Senate Finance Committee report measures against the **[Bank Secrecy Act](atlas-27921b10cb3cdde5dc90a6846d7d74b734dcb0c276d434b5ccbfd41bab9a4381.md)**'s "knows, suspects, or has reason to suspect" filing standard[^2] and 30-day filing deadline[^3]. At JPMorgan Chase, flagged activity totaled $4.3M across 2002–2016, against $1.3B flagged retroactively in August–September 2019 (post-arrest).[^4]

## Overview

The cited Senate reports and civil complaint describe inadequate or late suspicious activity reporting in the Epstein record.[^1] The page groups the cited reporting into two periods:

1. **Pre-arrest under-filing.** During the active client relationship, the bank files few SARs despite contemporaneous internal red flags[^5] (suspected cash structuring[^6], Butterfly-Trust beneficiary distributions[^7], ATTORNEY-1 cash-withdrawal patterns[^8], etc.).
2. **Post-arrest retroactive surge.** After Epstein's July 2019 arrest, the institution retroactively reports a disproportionate value and number of suspicious transactions — sometimes orders of magnitude more than during the active-relationship period.[^9] A Senate Finance Committee staff memorandum to Ranking Member Wyden treated the post-arrest surge as evidence supporting its concern that pre-arrest reporting was inadequate; the memorandum does not quote JPMorgan admitting inadequacy.[^10]

- **[The Senate Finance Committee staff memorandum](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/memorandum_to_senator_wyden_on_jpmc-epstein_redactedpdf.pdf)** reports $4.3M in JPMorgan Chase transactions flagged during 2002–2016 versus $1.3B flagged retroactively in August–September 2019.[^4]

JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.*, No. 1:22-cv-10904-JSR (S.D.N.Y.), ECF No. 187-1, Exhibit 1, filed June 15, 2023 (34 PDF pages; transcript cover at PDF p. 2).** — the held 34-page public excerpt contains no SAR or BSA testimony and does not substantiate a SAR-failure claim; its cover states that confidential BSA portions were filed separately[^11]
- **[*Jane Doe v. Bank of America, N.A.* Class Action Complaint, No. 1:25-cv-08520-JSR (S.D.N.Y. filed October 15, 2025), ECF No. 1.](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.651138/gov.uscourts.nysd.651138.1.0.pdf)** — SAR failure is the core Bank of America (BofA)-side civil allegation[^12]
- **Deutsche Bank** — see [Suspected Epstein Financial Offenses](atlas-030885beb2bad9dcb8e273d9251f7c1aa7d3bbc0f7911080016499eec7ea3714.md) for the cash-withdrawal pattern, which the Wyden report treats as a failure to flag in a timely manner, although the NYDFS consent order records that the Bank filed the required currency transaction reports (CTRs) and monitored the activity for suspicious activity reporting and makes no finding on SAR filing[^15]; the consent order records ATTORNEY-1's 97 withdrawals at $7,500 each[^8] and a July 2017 withdrawal split over two days after a teller confirmed that more than $10,000 would be reported[^13]; the bank's anti-money laundering (AML) staff said the pattern gave the appearance of structuring[^6], ATTORNEY-1 said he had not intended to structure, and bank personnel found him credible[^14]
