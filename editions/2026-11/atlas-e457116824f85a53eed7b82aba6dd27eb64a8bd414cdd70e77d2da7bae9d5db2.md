# COINTELPRO

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI)'s Counter Intelligence Program (1956–1971) — a series of covert operations targeting domestic political organizations, using surveillance, infiltration, discrediting, and disruption tactics against domestic groups.[^1]

## Overview

COINTELPRO ran from 1956 to 1971 under Director J. Edgar Hoover.[^2] It was not a single program but a series of named sub-programs targeting specific groups:[^3]

- **COINTELPRO-CPUSA (1956)** — Communist Party USA[^4]
- **COINTELPRO-SWP (1961)** — Socialist Workers Party[^5]
- **COINTELPRO-White Hate Groups (1964)** — Ku Klux Klan (KKK) and white supremacist organizations[^6]
- **COINTELPRO-Black Nationalist Hate Groups (1967)** — Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), Congress of Racial Equality (CORE), Nation of Islam, Black Panther Party, and specifically <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Martin Luther King Jr.</span>[^7]
- **COINTELPRO-New Left (1968)** — Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), antiwar groups, campus organizations[^8]

The program was exposed in 1971 after the FBI's resident agency in Media, Pennsylvania was broken into and the stolen documents were circulated and published by the press.[^9] Public exposure ended the formal program,[^10] but the Church Committee warned that COINTELPRO-type activities "may continue today under the rubric of 'investigation'" and found at least three post-1971 instances.[^11]

## Church Committee Findings

The [Church Committee](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/sites-default-files-94755-ii.pdf)'s Book II (*Intelligence Activities and the Rights of Americans*, 1976) sets out the Committee's findings on COINTELPRO; the detailed record is the COINTELPRO staff report in Book III.[^12] Key findings[^12]:

**Tactics documented by the Church Committee (Book II and the Book III COINTELPRO staff report):**[^12]

- Mailing anonymous letters to create suspicion between leaders and members[^13]
- Sending anonymous letters to spouses to create marital problems[^14]
- Notifying employers of political activities to cause job loss[^15]
- Leaking derogatory information to "friendly" journalists[^16]
- Obtaining targets' tax returns from the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) and trying to use IRS investigations or audits against individual targets (a protest leader; a professor)[^17]
- Using informants to raise controversial issues at meetings to cause dissent[^18]
- Falsely labeling group members as informants (the "snitch jacket"), which exposed them to expulsion or physical attack[^19]

**Regarding <span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Martin Luther King Jr.</span> specifically:**

- The FBI anonymously mailed King a surveillance tape and threatening letter that, his associates have said, he interpreted as an effort to induce him to commit suicide[^20]
- The Church Committee found that the FBI tried to undermine ambassadorial receptions during King's Nobel trip and later efforts to honor him[^21]
- On January 8, 1964, Assistant Director William C. Sullivan proposed that the FBI promote a new national civil-rights leader as King's successor once King had been discredited.[^22] Hoover approved exploring the plan, according to the Committee's staff report ([Book III](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/sites-default-files-94755-iii.pdf)), which found it "uncertain whether the FBI took steps to implement Sullivan's plan"; the proposed successor told the Committee he was never contacted by the FBI[^23]

**Lack of authorization:** The Church Committee found "no clear answer" to whether anyone outside the Bureau knew of COINTELPRO: the FBI produced documents showing that Attorneys General, presidential advisers, a House appropriations subcommittee and the Cabinet were put on notice of the CPUSA and White Hate programs, but none showing notice of the other three.[^24] The Committee judged many COINTELPRO proposals "clearly illegal", but the head of the FBI's Racial Intelligence Section testified that no one discussed the programs' legal authority: "No, we never gave it a thought."[^25] A 1974 Justice Department memorandum stated that COINTELPRO activities "may have violated" the civil rights, mail and wire fraud, and wiretap-disclosure statutes.[^26]

**Concealment:** Internal FBI documents required that each operation "be designed to protect the Bureau's interest so that there is no possibility of embarrassment to the Bureau."[^27] The FBI kept records of some illegal techniques outside its normal files under a "DO NOT FILE" procedure, and at least one such memorandum was moved from Hoover's "Personal Files" to his "Official and Confidential" files shortly before his death.[^28]

Carter administration charged Gray, Felt, and Edward S. Miller for illegal entries.[^29]

## Comparison with Hoover's Private Files

Where COINTELPRO used covert disruption against political organizations, Hoover kept some sensitive memoranda in his "Official and Confidential" and "Personal" files, and the Church Committee found that the widespread and accurate belief that the Bureau held derogatory information on politicians and critics created a "fear" of the Bureau.[^30]

## Relationship to Central Intelligence Agency Domestic Surveillance

COINTELPRO's FBI focus on domestic organizations ran parallel to Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)'s **<span class="link-unavailable" title="Not published in this edition">Operation CHAOS</span>** (1967–1974), which the Church Committee also documented: the CIA's Operation CHAOS, set up to find foreign links to racial, antiwar and other protest activity in the United States, which expanded its coverage of Americans; the staff report found that if the statutory ban on CIA 'internal security functions' is read broadly, CHAOS's basic mission violated the CIA's charter.[^31]
