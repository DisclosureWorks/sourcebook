# Office of Policy Coordination (OPC)

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)'s Covert Action operational unit, established as the "Office of Special Projects" by National Security Council (NSC) 10/2 (June 18, 1948) and soon renamed the Office of Policy Coordination, that conducted U.S. covert operations from 1948 until its merger with the Office of Special Operations in 1952.[^1]

## Overview

NSC 10/2 (June 18, 1948) established the Office of Special Projects within the CIA under the Director of Central Intelligence's control, with policy guidance from State and Defense and a chief nominated by the Secretary of State, acceptable to the Director of Central Intelligence, and approved by the National Security Council.[^2] **Frank Wisner** served as OPC's first chief;[^3] **George F. Kennan**, Director of the State Department's Policy Planning Staff, served as the State Department's representative on the 10/2 Panel from 1948 to 1950 and said in August 1948 that State would decide whether individual projects were politically desirable, although the Church Committee found that the State and Defense representatives had no approval function.[^4]

OPC grew from 302 personnel in 1949 to about 6,000 by 1952 (2,812 staff plus 3,142 overseas contract personnel),[^5] reflecting the scale of the U.S. covert response to early Cold War crises, above all the Korean War, and including support for resistance movements in Eastern Europe.[^6] In 1952, OPC was merged with the Office of Special Operations (CIA's clandestine intelligence collection arm) to form the Directorate of Plans (DDP) — the institutional unit that would conduct covert operations for the next 21 years until renamed the Directorate of Operations in 1973.[^7]

The Church Committee reported that the National Security Act contained no express reference to covert action, that Section 102(d)(5) was cited as its basis, and that Congress as a whole had never ratified covert operations by appropriation.[^8]

Book IV (Anne Karalekas's history of the CIA) gives personnel, budget and station counts for 1949 and 1952 (pp. 31-32), and Book I reports major covert operations in 48 countries by 1953 (p. 145).[^9] The number of operations, their geographic spread beyond station counts, and casualties remain open questions. The record also gives two different figures for OPC's budget in 1952: $82,000,000 (Book IV p. 31; Book I p. 107) and 'almost $200 million' when the Directorate of Plans was organized (Book I p. 146).[^10]

## Open Questions

- Did the Office of Special Projects become the Office of Policy Coordination almost immediately after NSC 10/2?
- What were the number of OPC operations, their geographic distribution beyond station counts, and their casualties during 1948–1952?
- What accounts for the different reported figures for OPC's 1952 budget?
