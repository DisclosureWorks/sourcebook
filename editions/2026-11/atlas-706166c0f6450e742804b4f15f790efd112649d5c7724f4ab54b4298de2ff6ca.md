# Surveillance Doctrine

**Assessed.** *This page states a conclusion. Our assessment is below.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The body of constitutional and statutory rules governing electronic surveillance by the U.S. government, comprising Fourth Amendment doctrine, the federal wiretap statutes (Title III of the 1968 Omnibus Crime Control Act and FISA 1978), and the constitutional architecture established by the *Katz* / *Keith* / FISA chain.[^1]

## Overview

This page treats the doctrine in three parts:

1. **Fourth Amendment baseline:** *Katz v. United States* (1967) established that warrantless electronic surveillance is presumptively unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment, subject to judicially recognized exceptions.[^2] *Katz* footnote 23 expressly reserved the question of warrantless surveillance for national-security purposes.[^3]

2. **Domestic-security warrant requirement:** *United States v. United States District Court (Keith)* (1972) closed the *Katz* footnote-23 reservation for domestic-security surveillance,[^4] requiring prior judicial authorization.[^5] The Court explicitly declined to address foreign-power surveillance,[^6] leaving that question open; courts of appeals then addressed it until Congress settled it by statute in FISA (1978).[^7]

3. **Statutory architecture:** Title III of the Omnibus Crime Control Act (1968) established the criminal-investigation wiretap regime;[^8] the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA, 1978) established the foreign-intelligence regime via the FISA Court.[^9] On this page's reading, the statutory regimes complement (and partially constrain) the constitutional architecture.

For the post-9/11 STELLARWIND program (NSA surveillance program under presidential authorization, 2001–2007, whose bulk-metadata components moved to FISA Court orders in 2004 and 2006),[^10] the Wyden-Udall correspondence and the PCLOB Section 215 report (2014) are the primary-record critique of its FISC-authorized successor, the Section 215 bulk telephone records program.[^11]

## Analytical significance

In the cases held here, challenges to the post-9/11 surveillance programs were resolved on state-secrets, standing or statutory grounds, or met the third-party doctrine, without a final appellate ruling on their Fourth Amendment merits.[^20] State-secrets-privilege dismissal can preempt merits review;[^12] standing barriers exclude individual plaintiffs;[^13] the *Smith v. Maryland* third-party doctrine, as most courts have read it, gives bulk metadata collection a constitutional safe harbor, although that reading was contested (one district court, in *Klayman v. Obama* (2013), held that *Smith* does not apply to the NSA program);[^14] and FISA Court proceedings were ex parte; Senators Wyden and Udall wrote in 2011 that the government relied on secret interpretations of surveillance authorities that, in their judgment, differ significantly from the public's understanding.[^15] The Church Committee Book II (1976) documented the pre-FISA architecture;[^16] the post-Snowden litigation tested the third-party doctrine against bulk collection, with divided results.[^17]

> **Strongest counterargument:** Courts did reach the merits: the Eastern District of Michigan held the Terrorist Surveillance Program unconstitutional in 2006, and Klayman v. Obama (2013) held that Smith does not apply to bulk telephone metadata.

> **Response:** Neither is a final appellate merits ruling. The Sixth Circuit vacated the Michigan judgment for lack of standing; Klayman was a preliminary-injunction ruling, which the D.C. Circuit vacated in 2015 on standing-related grounds without reaching Smith (not held here). The Second Circuit in ACLU v. Clapper decided on statutory grounds without reaching the constitutional arguments. Confidence is medium because the set of held cases is not exhaustive.

## Open Questions

- **Question:** Given the NSA Inspector General’s account of letters invoking presidential Article II authority with the Attorney General’s legal concurrence and the joint inspectors-general report’s account of an emergency authorization for surveillance without a court order,[^18] is STELLARWIND the defining example of an executive claim to inherent authority outside the *Katz* / *Keith* / FISA architecture, or one instance among others?
- **Question:** Given PCLOB’s account of opposing district-court outcomes on *Smith* and bulk telephone records,[^19] and the Second Circuit’s statutory disposition without reaching the constitutional issues in *ACLU v. Clapper* (2015),[^20] which appellate decisions, if any, resolved the constitutional merits, and which instead resolved standing, mootness, statutory, or state-secrets issues?
