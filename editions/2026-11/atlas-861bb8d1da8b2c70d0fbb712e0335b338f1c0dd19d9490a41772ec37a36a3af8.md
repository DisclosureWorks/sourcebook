# Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA)

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The 1978 federal statute (P.L. 95-511; 50 U.S.C. § 1801 et seq.)[^1] establishing a statutory warrant regime for foreign-intelligence electronic surveillance, with the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court (FISC) as the issuing body,[^2] addressing, for foreign intelligence, the national-security question *Katz* footnote 23 reserved and *Keith* expressly declined to reach.[^3]

## Overview

FISA was enacted October 25, 1978[^4] as the post-Watergate / post-Church-Committee statutory response to the doctrinal gap left by *United States v. United States District Court (Keith)* (1972).[^5] *Keith* required a Fourth Amendment warrant for domestic-security surveillance[^6] but expressly reserved the foreign-power question;[^7] FISA provided the answer: a specialized Article III court (the FISC) issues surveillance orders after a specialized probable-cause inquiry into foreign-power or agent status and the targeted facility, rather than Title III's ordinary-crime formulation.[^8]

The original 1978 architecture had two main ex parte components: the FISC (initially seven district-court judges; expanded to eleven in 2001) issuing warrants, and the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court of Review hearing government appeals of FISC denials.[^9] Original FISA §201(b), codified at 18 U.S.C. §2511(2)(f), made FISA and the criminal wiretap statute the exclusive means for covered electronic surveillance;[^10] 50 U.S.C. §1812 added a separate exclusivity provision in 2008.[^11]

The post-9/11 statutory architecture extended FISA substantially: the USA PATRIOT Act (2001),[^12] the FISA Amendments Act of 2008 (creating Section 702 for surveillance of non-U.S. persons reasonably believed to be located abroad),[^13] and the USA FREEDOM Act (2015, which ended Section 215 bulk-telephony collection).[^14]

## Later surveillance programs

The FISC's ex parte structure — the government appears, the surveilled does not, the proceedings are classified —[^15] means that no party opposing the government takes part before an application is approved; after an order has been issued, the statute and the FISC rules provide opportunities for recipients of such orders to challenge them, but such challenges are very rare.[^16] The Wyden-Udall correspondence (2011–2012) is an attempt by senators to challenge secret statutory interpretations;[^17] the PCLOB Section 215 report (2014) documents the Board majority's post-Snowden conclusion that Section 215 did not authorize the bulk-telephony program the FISC had approved, a conclusion two Board members (Brand and Collins Cook) did not join.[^18]

## Open Questions

- **Question:** Does the FISC's ex parte structure amount to a failure of fire-alarm oversight in the McCubbins–Schwartz sense — oversight that relies on citizens and interest groups outside government to raise the alarm — when the proceedings exclude the surveilled party and the proceedings are classified?[^20]
- **Question:** Annual reports to Congress on FISA applications (transmitted by the Attorney General under 50 U.S.C. § 1807) contain partial data;[^19] what denial, modification, and prefiling-revision rates can be established from those counts and later public records, and what would those rates show about the FISC’s review?
