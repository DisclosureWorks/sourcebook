# Intelligence Budget Secrecy

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** The systematic concealment of true intelligence expenditure from Congress through misrepresentation, omission, reclassification, and organizational fragmentation.[^1] The Pike Committee found, for example, that the first total of federal domestic-intelligence costs, compiled by the Office of Management and Budget at the Committee's request, was "more than five times" the amount given to the Committee in testimony.[^2]

## Overview

Its findings documented multiple distinct methods by which intelligence expenditure was kept out of the official totals, so that neither Congress nor government auditors could determine actual intelligence expenditure.[^3]

The Pike Committee found that there was no single budget figure that captured total intelligence spending, and suggested this may have been deliberate.[^4] Multiple agencies applied their own budget standards with no central authority capable of enforcing consistent definitions.[^5] As the Director of Central Intelligence acknowledged, there was no agreed definition of "intelligence" within the government.[^6]

## Concealment Techniques Documented

**Stated vs. actual totals.** GAO accountants on loan to the Pike Committee concluded that the foreign-intelligence budget was three to four times more costly than Congress had been told.[^7] An OMB review of the domestic intelligence budget, conducted at the committee's request, found costs may be 5x higher than stated.[^8] A 1971 Schlesinger/OMB study concluded the foreign intelligence budget was "nearly double the amount being told to Congress."[^9]

**NSA omission.** The National Security Agency (NSA) — which did "nothing else except gather and analyze technical intelligence" — had roughly 20 percent of its budget excluded from the intelligence aggregate presented to Congress.[^10]

**Transfer and reclassification.** DoD transferred items from the intelligence budget to "Communications," "Research and Development," and other categories, shifting hundreds of millions of dollars and thousands of personnel.[^11] The Advance Location Strike Program, Mapping/Charting/Geodesy, and Counterintelligence and Investigative Activities were all reclassified.[^12] Entire agencies (Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency) were omitted from intelligence-related cost estimates.[^13]

**Undervaluation.** In one covert operation the Committee analyzed, CIA valued the weapons supplied at about half their Defense Department contract prices.[^14] Submarine repair costs and military base support for intelligence operations were not allocated to intelligence budgets under any consistent standard, and some intelligence activities were reclassified as research and development.[^15]

**Reprogramming without approval.** CIA's budget appeared as a single line item in the published federal budget ("secrecy").[^16] CIA could then tell Congress it was spending a certain amount on covert action, then "proceed to transfer large amounts to covert programs without Congress' approval."[^17] This gave CIA "an unusual advantage" over other agencies subject to congressional reprogramming restrictions.[^18]

**Agency fragmentation.** The Director of Central Intelligence (DCI) presented the entire foreign intelligence budget but had authority only over CIA's budget; Defense officials testified that a "substantial part" of their intelligence budget was the responsibility of the Secretary of Defense, and that the DCI "merely reviews their work."[^19] FBI did not know whether CIA had a counterintelligence group, how much CIA's operations cost, or whether CIA duplicated FBI work.[^20]

## GAO Exclusion

The Pike Report states that from 1962 the General Accounting Office (renamed the Government Accountability Office in 2004)[^33], Congress's auditor, made no attempt to audit the CIA because it was allowed only "scant access" to classified spending.[^21] According to the Pike Report, when GAO was directed to compile basic budgetary information on federal investigative and intelligence functions, it was refused information by CIA, NSA and Defense Department intelligence agencies; GAO's own letter in the hearing record says the request came from the Senate Government Operations and Senate Budget Committees and that OMB refused the data for CIA, NSA and certain other sensitive Defense intelligence activities.[^22] The FBI allowed GAO to examine summaries but refused to permit verification of those summaries.[^23]

Paradoxically, the CIA gave a private consulting firm (Peat Marwick Livingston & Co.) full access to all classified procurement documents and personnel in 1966.[^24] CIA could have saved money by giving GAO that same access; instead it hired the private firm for $55,725.[^25]

## CIA Internal Audit Failures

CIA's own audit staff was undermanned for a comprehensive review of the agency's worldwide spending.[^26] Only 5% of vouchered transactions were checked, even though these represented 20% of CIA's entire budget.[^27] Auditors were not always allowed to know the exact purpose of expenditures.[^28] Auditor recommendations were "usually not disclosed" in the auditor's report to headquarters.[^29] Overseas stations were not audited on a regular basis; it could be two or three years, or more, before a station was audited.[^30] The CIA's own internal standards deviated from Certified Public Accountant standards, and no list of permitted deviations was maintained.[^31]

## Policy Significance

The Pike Report found that keeping the intelligence budget total secret was in "direct conflict" with the U.S. Constitution's requirement to publish "a [regular] Statement and Account of the Receipts and Expenditures of all public Money" (Art. I, Sec. 9).[^32]
