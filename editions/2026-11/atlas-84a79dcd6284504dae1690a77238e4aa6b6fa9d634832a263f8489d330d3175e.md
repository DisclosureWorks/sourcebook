# Grand Jury Secrecy (Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 6(e))

**Reference.** *Reports what the sources say. States no conclusion of ours.*

Edition label: `2026-11`

> Not everything on this page is footnoted. Our citation check covers the main text, but not headings, the lists of related entries and sources, or open questions, which may state facts without a footnote. Check the footnoted text or the source before relying on them.

**Definition:** Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 6(e) establishes the default regime of grand-jury secrecy — prohibiting disclosure of "a matter occurring before the grand jury" by attorneys, jurors, interpreters, court reporters, and other personnel, subject to Rule 6(e)'s exceptions; the cited 2025 opinion also describes a Second and Seventh Circuit doctrine permitting disclosure in special circumstances outside the rule.[^1]

## Overview

Rule 6(e) exists to protect grand jury deliberation from disclosure — shielding investigative targets not ultimately charged from public exposure.[^2] Disclosure exceptions are narrow: use by government attorneys in performing their duties (6(e)(3)(A)), grand jury testimony for use in another court proceeding (6(e)(3)(E)(i)), and a handful of other structured categories.[^3] Since the Eleventh Circuit's 2020 en banc decision in Pitch v. United States, courts in that circuit have treated Rule 6(e)(3)'s exceptions as exhaustive; earlier circuit law allowed disclosure outside them.[^4]

## The Epstein Files Transparency Act Override

The **Epstein Files Transparency Act** (Nov 2025)[^5] presented a test of whether a later-enacted, case-specific federal statute can override the general Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 6(e) prohibition.[^6] Two unsealing requests produced opposing rulings in the same court; only the second was decided under the Act, which was enacted after the first ruling:[^7]

- **Rosenberg denial (July 23, 2025):** held that under Eleventh Circuit law "the Court's hands are tied" absent an enumerated 6(e) exception.[^8]
- **Smith grant (December 5, 2025):** held that "the later-enacted and specific language of the Act trumps Rule 6’s prohibition on disclosure."[^9]

Two S.D.N.Y. judges in the Second Circuit reached the same result days later: United States v. Maxwell (ECF 820, Dec. 9, 2025) and United States v. Epstein (ECF 92, Dec. 10, 2025);[^10] both are district-court orders, and this page reports no appellate ruling on the theory but rests on no comprehensive appellate docket search.

## Open Questions

- As of April 2026, had any circuit court affirmed or rejected the EFTA-overrides-Rule-6(e) theory?

- Was the December 5, 2025 Smith order the first federal ruling to adopt an EFTA-overrides-Rule-6(e) theory?

- Will the EFTA-overrides-6(e) theory survive appellate review if challenged by the Epstein estate, Maxwell, or any defense-team successor-in-interest?
- Does the Smith theory generalize — would a future case-specific statute similarly override Rule 6(e)?
- How did the Eleventh Circuit's 2020 en banc *Pitch* decision change the disclosure practice permitted under its earlier *Hastings* precedent?
- What does EFTA's enactment between the July and December 2025 rulings imply for comparisons of the different legal questions those rulings decided?
